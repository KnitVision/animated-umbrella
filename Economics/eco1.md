# Economics 1: introduction to schools of thought

> [!summary] Main idea
> Economics studies the economy through competing theories. This course introduces Marxian, Keynesian, and neoclassical approaches, with attention to what each treats as its basic unit of analysis. The lecture also places those approaches in a rough history of economic thought.

## What this part of the course covers

The economics and political science portions of the course are largely independent. The economics portion has about 14 lectures and three broad modules:

1. **Introduction and Marxian economics.** Why economists use different schools of thought, followed by the basics of a Marxian account of capitalism.
2. **Keynesian economics and macroeconomics.** Economy-wide output and employment, inflation, unemployment, and related policy questions.
3. **Neoclassical economics and microeconomics.** The choices of individual consumers and producers, including firms.

The course is mainly about *economic theory*: the frameworks used to explain economic events. It will refer to real events, but it is not a survey of current economic affairs. The lecturer's hope is that these frameworks will help students think more carefully about events they encounter later, including new developments such as AI.

## Economy, economics, and schools of thought

- The **economy** is the real activity and set of relationships being studied.
- **Economics** is the structured attempt to understand and explain that activity.
- A **school of thought** is a way of framing economic questions and building explanations. Economics has several such approaches, rather than one agreed way to interpret every event.

The distinction resembles history: events happen, while historians use ideas and methods to make sense of them. Here, the focus is on *how* economists explain economic affairs, rather than on memorizing a sequence of affairs.

### Microeconomics and macroeconomics

| Perspective | Main unit of analysis | Typical question in this lecture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Microeconomics | Individual consumers and producers or firms | Why does an economic agent make a particular choice? |
| Macroeconomics | The economy as a whole | How are total output and employment determined? |

The lecturer associates the course's microeconomics module mainly with neoclassical economics and its macroeconomics module mainly with Keynesian economics. These pairs are useful for this course, but the terms are not exact synonyms. A macroeconomic explanation can study an outcome at the level of the whole economy without deriving it directly from each person's decision.

## A rough timeline of economic thought

This is a teaching outline, not a claim that one school ended when another began. They overlap and continue to change.

| Period | Development | Point to remember |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 16th–17th centuries | Sustained analysis of economic affairs within the European tradition | Calling this the *origin* of economic thought leaves out non-European traditions. |
| 1776 | Adam Smith publishes *An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations* | Often treated as a starting point for modern economics. |
| Early 19th century | Classical political economy develops through writers including David Ricardo and Thomas Malthus | Social classes and their economic interests are central to its analysis. |
| 1867 onward | The first volume of Karl Marx's *Capital* appears | Marx develops an extensive analysis and critique of capitalism. |
| Late 19th century | Neoclassical economics emerges | Analysis turns toward individual agents and makes greater use of mathematics. |
| Early 20th century | Economics becomes more established as a university discipline | Neoclassical economics becomes dominant in many curricula. |
| 1929–1930s | The Great Depression prompts renewed questions about market self-correction | The crisis forms the setting for Keynes's work. |
| 1936 | John Maynard Keynes publishes *The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money* | Economy-wide output, employment, and public policy move to the foreground. |
| 1970s onward | Keynesian predictions face criticism; neoclassical approaches regain influence | Neoclassical economics remains influential, while continuing to evolve. |

The lecturer explicitly notes the **Eurocentric** nature of the conventional starting point. Economic thinking existed outside Europe and before the 16th century. This timeline follows the standard history taught in many university economics courses, not a complete history of thought about economic life.

### Smith, Ricardo, and Malthus

**Adam Smith.** The lecture uses Smith's *invisible hand* to introduce confidence in markets: decentralized exchanges can coordinate economic activity without a central authority directing every choice. This is the familiar account of Smith discussed in class, not a full account of his work.

**David Ricardo.** Ricardo argued for gains from trade through **comparative advantage**. If countries specialize according to what they produce at a lower *relative* cost and trade, both can benefit. This argument supported freer trade at a time when trade restrictions were common.

**Thomas Malthus.** Malthus worried that population might grow faster than food production, producing famine. The lecturer says his predicted outcome did not occur in the form he feared, pointing to falling birth rates and improvements in agricultural production. The distinction that matters for the course is that **enough food in total does not guarantee that everyone can obtain food**.

John Stuart Mill is also named as a classical political economist, though the lecture does not discuss his ideas.

### Marx and the rise of neoclassical economics

Marx's *Capital* examines a maturing capitalist system. The lecturer presents Marx as both connected to classical political economy and a major critic of it. Like the classical writers, Marx takes **classes with shared economic interests** seriously. The next lecture will develop his account in more detail.

Neoclassical economics emerged in the late 19th century and focused more closely on the decisions of **individual consumers and producers**. Mathematics became more prominent in economic analysis. The lecturer cautions against assuming that using mathematics automatically makes an explanation of society more convincing.

### Keynes and the Great Depression

The Great Depression challenged the belief that markets would reliably restore good economic outcomes on their own. Keynes studied **business cycles**, the rises and falls in economy-wide output. When output falls, employment can fall too, producing unemployment. He argued that a market economy can remain in trouble and that **government policy may be needed** to raise activity and employment.

The lecture presents Keynesian economics as influential through the mid-20th century. From the 1970s, doubts about some Keynesian predictions helped neoclassical approaches regain prominence. The lecturer stresses that today's neoclassical economics has changed substantially since its beginnings.

## How the approaches differ

| Approach | Main unit of analysis | Emphasis in this lecture |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Classical political economy | Social classes, such as capitalists, workers, and landlords | How their distinct interests and relationships shape the economy. |
| Marxian economics | Classes and the organization of production | How capitalism works through productive resources and social relationships. |
| Keynesian economics | The economy as a whole | How total output and employment change, and what policy can do during a downturn. |
| Neoclassical economics | Individual consumers and producers or firms | How agents with defined goals make choices. |

For this course, **classical economics** and **classical political economy** refer to roughly the same tradition. The broader term *political economy* is also used by later approaches, so context matters.

The lecturer briefly introduces two Marxian terms, with more detail promised next time:

- **Forces of production:** the means and capacities used to produce, including tools, machines, technology, and labor.
- **Relations of production:** the social and institutional relationships governing production, including ownership and employment relationships.

## Why do several schools persist?

One explanation is that new theories respond to shortcomings in older ones. That alone does not explain why older approaches remain in use. The lecturer's further point is that economic theories start from different questions and assumptions about people, society, and how economies work.

Evidence matters, but social sciences cannot always settle disagreements through controlled experiments. Mathematics, statistics, and data can support different accounts of the same event. People's prior experiences and views also affect which explanation they find convincing. Those views can change with study and experience.

The useful habit is to ask: **What is this theory trying to explain, what does it assume, and does its account fit the case at hand?** The dominance of one school does not, by itself, answer those questions.

## Quick review

1. What is the difference between an economy and economics?
2. What does each school treat as its main unit of analysis?
3. How does comparative advantage support Ricardo's argument for trade?
4. Why does adequate food production not necessarily prevent hunger?
5. What did the Great Depression reveal about the limits of market self-correction in Keynes's account?
6. Why might different economic theories persist even when one is dominant?
