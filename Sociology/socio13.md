# Urban marginality, labour restructuring, and suicide

> [!summary]
> A city does not become more equal simply because planners call it modern. Zarin Ahmad's study of Delhi's abattoirs shows how relocation, mechanization, and sanitation policy displaced workers, broke occupational networks, raised costs, and produced new informal work. Jonathan Parry's study of suicide in Bhilai makes a related argument about economic liberalization. The loss of secure public-sector employment threatened the class position of a labour aristocracy and made downward mobility a collective crisis. Both studies connect apparently individual outcomes to changes in work, space, technology, and state policy.

## Division of labour and division of labourers

The lecture begins from Ambedkar's distinction in [[socio11|Social stratification: class, caste, and life chances]]. A complex production process requires a **division of labour**. People perform different tasks and develop different skills. This alone does not require permanent social segregation.

A **division of labourers** fixes people to occupations and ranks those occupations unequally. The worker cannot easily learn another task, leave the occupation, or escape the social identity attached to it. Caste makes this closure hereditary.

| Division of labour | Division of labourers |
|---|---|
| Work is divided into specialized tasks. | Workers are divided into ranked groups. |
| Specialization may change with training. | Birth restricts entry, exit, and mobility. |
| The task is differentiated. | The worker's social worth is classified. |
| It can exist in many economic systems. | It reproduces durable inequality. |

The distinction matters in both readings. Ahmad studies occupational communities whose work is tied to caste and religion. Parry studies workers whose class position once depended on permanent employment and became difficult to reproduce after liberalization.

## Urban space is socially organized

Classical urban models describe a city growing around a **central business district**, or CBD. Businesses occupy valuable central land. Many owners and affluent residents live at a distance from crowded commercial and industrial zones, while workers need affordable housing near their jobs.

This pattern produces spatial inequality:

```text
Valuable commercial centre
          ↓
Demand for nearby labour
          ↓
Dense and cheaper worker housing
          ↓
Affluent housing at a protected distance
```

The arrangement is never only about distance. Elevation, drainage, roads, transport, rent, and exposure to pollution shape where people can afford to live. Two neighbourhoods may appear equally connected to a main road, yet the lower-lying side may flood during the monsoon and therefore command lower rent.

Planned enclaves also depend on workers who are kept socially and visually outside them. Domestic workers, delivery workers, drivers, cleaners, construction workers, and security guards must live close enough to supply daily labour. They often settle in cheaper lanes, older villages, or informal colonies beside wealthier developments.

> [!important]
> Urban segregation does not place rich and poor in completely separate worlds. It places them close enough for labour to circulate, while keeping housing, sanitation, safety, and prestige sharply unequal.

## Caste beyond a narrow account of Hindu doctrine

The lecture rejects the claim that caste exists only among Hindus. Caste-like rank, endogamy, and hereditary occupation also shape Muslim, Sikh, and Christian communities in South Asia. Religious doctrine may oppose hierarchy while social practice reproduces it.

The example of Dalit Sikhs being excluded from some gurdwaras shows the gap between doctrine and practice. The *Guru Granth Sahib* contains strong anti-caste currents, yet caste can persist in worship, settlement, marriage, and everyday interaction. The growth of deras in Punjab must be read partly against this exclusion. They may provide religious and social spaces to communities denied dignity elsewhere.

Among Muslims, categories such as **Ashraf**, **Ajlaf**, and **Arzal**, as well as occupational biradaris, show that religious equality does not automatically erase inherited social rank. The Qureshi community in Delhi is important to Ahmad's study because butchery has become an intergenerational occupation and a stigmatized identity.

## Zarin Ahmad on Delhi's meat economy

Zarin Ahmad studies Muslim butchers and other workers connected to Delhi's meat economy. Her article **"Marginal Occupations and Modernising Cities: Muslim Butchers in Urban India"** follows the relocation of the Idgah abattoir to a mechanized plant in Ghazipur.

The case looks like a narrow dispute about one slaughterhouse. It is actually about urban planning, religious politics, caste, technology, labour, and the effort to make Delhi appear like a clean, orderly, "world-class" city.

### The Idgah abattoir as a labour network

The old Idgah abattoir was more than a building. Decades of work had produced a network around it:

- Qureshi butchers brought specialized knowledge of slaughter and meat preparation.
- Dalit workers handled cleaning and animal remains and recovered hides and other saleable parts.
- Traders brought animals to the site and moved meat to shops and markets.
- Transporters, retailers, waste workers, and leather traders depended on one another.
- Nearby households and markets supported the daily movement of people and goods.

This organization contained inequality. Occupations were marked by caste, religion, and stigma. But relocation could not remove that inequality merely by moving the building. It disrupted a working system without guaranteeing alternative livelihoods.

```text
Abattoir
   ├── butchers and meat cutters
   ├── cleaners and sanitation workers
   ├── livestock traders
   ├── transporters
   ├── hide and leather workers
   ├── meat retailers
   └── households and markets nearby
```

When the state relocates the abattoir, every connection must either move, be rebuilt, or disappear.

### Sanitation as an urban and political argument

The demand to close Idgah was presented through the language of congestion, pollution, sanitation, and modernization. These concerns were not invented from nothing. The old site had real infrastructure problems. Ahmad's point is that planners treated removal as if it were the same as reform.

The politics of meat made the case more charged. Beef and buffalo meat sit at the intersection of religious identity, majoritarian claims, livelihood, and consumption. A call for sanitation could therefore carry a second demand: remove a stigmatized occupation and its workers from the visible city.

Food practice also varies within religions. Bengali Hindu traditions that offer fish or meat to a goddess do not fit a single vegetarian definition of Hindu identity. Claims about what a "real" Hindu must eat turn one regional practice into a universal religious test.

> [!note]
> The sociological question is not whether hygiene matters. It is why hygiene is pursued through the displacement of particular workers, and why the new arrangement may reproduce the same hazard elsewhere.

### Relocation to Ghazipur

The new mechanized abattoir was built in Ghazipur on Delhi's eastern edge, near a major landfill. This exposed the contradiction in the official justification.

| Official claim | Material outcome |
|---|---|
| Move slaughter out of the old city for hygiene. | Place the new facility beside a landfill with serious air, water, and waste problems. |
| Modernize production through machinery. | Exclude workers whose skills or numbers no longer fit the new assembly line. |
| Create an orderly and regulated meat economy. | Raise travel and processing costs, encouraging unlicensed slaughter elsewhere. |
| Improve urban space. | Shift pollution and stigmatized work toward the margin. |

Workers who lived near Idgah now had to travel across the city. Animals also required motorized transport. Distance increased the cost of labour, fuel, and access to the plant.

### Mechanization, deskilling, and exclusion

Mechanization reorganized slaughter as an assembly line. A worker who once understood much of the process might now repeat one small task. Machines reduced the number of workers required and demanded different skills.

The result combined several changes:

- **Deskilling:** existing craft knowledge lost value when work was split into narrower operations.
- **Reskilling:** the new plant required workers who could operate within a mechanized process.
- **Unemployment:** the plant absorbed far fewer people than the older labour-intensive system.
- **Higher costs:** fees, transport, and formal processing made legal slaughter more expensive.
- **Private control:** modernization transferred more authority over access and work to the plant's operator.

This resembles Marx's account of machinery in [[socio10|Social reproduction: care, automation, and labor control]]. Technology does not enter an empty workplace. Its effects depend on ownership, job security, training, and whether workers control the gains from higher productivity.

### Formalization can produce informality

The most striking contradiction is that a policy meant to formalize meat production can create more informal work.

```text
Relocation and mechanization
            ↓
Longer travel, fewer jobs, and higher fees
            ↓
Some workers cannot use the formal plant
            ↓
Unlicensed and dispersed slaughter grows
            ↓
Greater suspicion, policing, and communal tension
```

At Idgah, meat leaving a recognized facility carried institutional certification. When high costs push work outside that system, buyers and police may treat meat and its transporters with greater suspicion. In a climate of cow-protection vigilantism, informality can expose workers to harassment or lynching.

The policy therefore creates the condition it claims to eliminate. Order at the level of the master plan generates disorder in workers' daily lives.

## Jonathan Parry on suicide in Bhilai

Jonathan Parry's **"Suicide in a Central Indian Steel Town"** asks why suicide increased around Bhilai, a major public-sector steel town in Chhattisgarh. His answer shifts attention from individual pathology to economic restructuring and class reproduction.

### Bhilai and the labour aristocracy

Bhilai Steel Plant once offered secure public-sector employment across a graded occupational hierarchy. The company township organized housing and social life around employment rank. Permanent workers received wages, housing, benefits, and status unavailable to most informal workers.

Parry calls this relatively privileged group an **aristocracy of labour**. The phrase does not mean that they owned the plant or escaped exploitation. It means they occupied a protected position within the wider working population.

The local contrast between **naukri** and **kaam** captures this distinction:

- **Naukri** means stable, salaried employment with recognized status and benefits.
- **Kaam** means work, often casual, contractual, insecure, or lacking a recognized career position.

A person may work hard and earn money through *kaam* yet still lack the respect attached to *naukri*. Social prestige belongs to the employment relation, not simply to the activity performed.

### Liberalization and the shrinking of secure work

The lecture places Parry's findings in the context of India's 1991 economic reforms:

- **Liberalization** reduced state controls and permissions governing private economic activity.
- **Privatization** expanded private ownership or management and reduced the state's role in public enterprises.
- **Globalization** opened the economy further to international capital, trade, and competition.

At Bhilai, restructuring meant downsizing, mechanization, fewer new permanent posts, and more contract labour. Work continued, but secure jobs became harder to enter and reproduce across generations.

An employer could replace a permanent workforce with labour supplied by a contractor for a specific task. The plant avoided long-term obligations for wages, housing, pensions, and family security. Contract workers performed necessary labour without joining the protected company workforce.

### Downward social mobility

The loss of secure employment affected classes differently. Workers who had always occupied insecure positions faced worsening conditions, but they did not experience the same fall from a protected status. Families of permanent employees expected children to maintain or improve the class position created by the parent's job.

When that route closed, they confronted **downward social mobility**:

```text
Parent's permanent public-sector job
              ↓
Housing, income, benefits, and prestige
              ↓
Expectation that children will retain the position
              ↓
Downsizing and fewer permanent jobs
              ↓
Crisis of class reproduction
```

The crisis was relative as well as material. A family could remain better off than a contract worker's household while still experience a profound loss of future, standing, and identity.

### Employment, masculinity, and marriage

In the steel town, a permanent job made a young man a desirable marriage prospect. Without *naukri*, paid activity could be dismissed as mere *kaam*. Unemployment and insecure work therefore damaged income, masculine status, and the ability to form a household.

The lecture's example of a street musician makes the distinction plain. An audience may praise the performance and even pay the musician, then ask, "But what work do you do?" The question denies that the performance counts as proper employment.

Parry connects blocked marriage, indebtedness, alcohol use, family conflict, and status anxiety to the restructuring of work. These are not separate personal failures. They are ways an economic change enters intimate life.

## Durkheim and Marx on the same deaths

Parry's case can be read through both [[socio8|Emile Durkheim: social facts, solidarity, and regulation]] and Marx.

### A Durkheimian reading

Durkheim asks how **social integration** and **social regulation** affect suicide. Rapid economic change unsettles expectations about work, respect, marriage, and the future. The old rules lose their reliability while new ones fail to provide security. This resembles **anomie**.

A Durkheimian account would ask:

- Has restructuring weakened stable ties to workplace and community?
- Have aspirations and expectations become difficult to regulate?
- Do family and institutional pressures over-regulate some people?
- Does the loss of a recognized role reduce integration?

### A Marxian reading

A Marxian explanation begins with the transformation of the economic base. Liberalization, mechanization, downsizing, and contractualization alter how labour is organized. Changes in consciousness, family conflict, status, and despair follow from this reorganization of material life.

| Durkheim | Marx |
|---|---|
| Studies integration, regulation, and anomie. | Studies class relations and changes in production. |
| Asks how social bonds and norms organize a person's life. | Asks how restructuring removes security and changes class reproduction. |
| Treats suicide as a social fact. | Locates personal crisis within political economy. |

The approaches emphasize different mechanisms, but both reject the idea that suicide can be explained solely by a defective individual.

## Suicide records do not simply report reality

Parry also questions official evidence. Police records, family statements, and statistics do not provide a transparent account of motive. Families may fear shame, legal scrutiny, or police involvement. Officials may write a familiar explanation such as alcohol use or domestic conflict even when the evidence is weak.

The recorded cause is therefore a social product:

```text
Death
  + family fear and stigma
  + police procedure
  + legal categories
  + available public narratives
  = official account of suicide
```

This does not mean the record is useless. It means the sociologist must study how the record was made. Categories and statistics can hide the economic changes that made a death more likely.

> [!warning]
> A sociological explanation does not deny grief, mental illness, or personal agency. It asks why distress becomes concentrated in particular groups and periods, and why institutions describe it in particular ways.

## Applying the argument to academic life and AI

The lecture applies the same reasoning to academic misconduct and student suicide. An individual may choose to cheat, but sociology asks why grades carry such high stakes and why a particular method of cheating has become normal and available.

Grades shape internships, placements, scholarships, and status. A low score may be read as a forecast of a damaged future. Institutional discipline can then intensify the crisis through interrogation, suspension, shame, or isolation.

Generative AI creates another contradiction. Students encounter it throughout everyday work and may use it for summaries, drafting, or revision. Repeated dependence can weaken writing and recall. Institutions may still demand unaided performance without redesigning assessment or teaching the boundaries of acceptable use.

```text
High-stakes grading
        +
Easy access to generative AI
        +
Unclear or inconsistent rules
        +
Severe disciplinary pressure
        ↓
Risk is individualized as "a bad student"
```

The act still belongs to the student, but responsibility does not end there. The institution designs the assessment, decides the sanction, controls support, and sets the meaning attached to grades.

## What the two readings show together

Ahmad and Parry study different settings, but their arguments share a structure.

| Delhi's abattoirs | Bhilai Steel Plant |
|---|---|
| Relocation and mechanization reorganize a meat economy. | Liberalization and contractualization reorganize industrial work. |
| Workers lose access to an occupational network. | Families lose a path to secure employment. |
| Formalization produces new informal practices. | Formal employment gives way to insecure contract work. |
| Caste and religious stigma shape who bears the cost. | Class status shapes how insecurity is experienced. |
| Urban order is achieved through displacement. | Economic efficiency is achieved through downsizing. |

Both cases warn against judging a reform by its stated intention. The relevant question is what happens across the whole social system after costs, risks, and labour are redistributed.

1. A division of labour differentiates tasks, while a division of labourers fixes ranked groups to work.
2. Urban class relations appear in housing, drainage, rent, distance, transport, and access to services.
3. Planned affluent enclaves depend on nearby pools of lower-paid labour.
4. Caste and hereditary occupational ranking occur across South Asian religious communities.
5. Zarin Ahmad treats the abattoir as a network of workers, skills, markets, and social relations.
6. The relocation from Idgah to Ghazipur moved stigmatized work to the edge of Delhi without resolving sanitation.
7. Mechanization reduced jobs, devalued existing skills, raised costs, and excluded many workers.
8. A policy of formalization can produce informality when people cannot afford to enter the formal system.
9. Parry links rising suicide in Bhilai to liberalization and a crisis in reproducing class status.
10. The labour aristocracy enjoyed secure public-sector employment relative to contract and informal workers.
11. *Naukri* carries security and prestige, while *kaam* may mean work without a recognized social position.
12. Downward mobility affects income, family expectations, marriage, masculinity, and self-worth.
13. Durkheim explains suicide through integration and regulation. Marx directs attention to changes in production and class relations.
14. Police and family records construct official motives rather than merely discovering them.
15. Individual actions must be analyzed alongside the institutions and social structures that organize their consequences.

## Key terms

- **Division of labourers:** Hereditary or otherwise durable allocation of ranked groups to occupations.
- **Central business district:** A city's concentrated commercial and administrative core.
- **Urban segregation:** Unequal spatial distribution of groups, housing, services, hazards, and mobility.
- **Biradari:** A kinship or community grouping that can organize marriage, occupation, and mutual support.
- **Abattoir:** A regulated slaughterhouse.
- **Mechanization:** Replacement or reorganization of human labour through machinery.
- **Deskilling:** Reduction in the range or value of skills a worker uses.
- **Formalization:** Movement of work under official rules, registration, and regulation.
- **Labour aristocracy:** A relatively protected and privileged section of the working class.
- **Naukri:** Secure and socially recognized employment, especially salaried work.
- **Kaam:** Work or a task that may lack the security and status of formal employment.
- **Contractualization:** Replacement of permanent jobs with fixed-term or contractor-supplied labour.
- **Downward social mobility:** Movement to a less secure, lower-income, or lower-status social position.
- **Class reproduction:** The process through which a family maintains its class position across generations.
- **Anomie:** Weakening or disruption of the norms that regulate expectations and conduct.

## Assigned-reading connections

**Zarin Ahmad, "Marginal Occupations and Modernising Cities: Muslim Butchers in Urban India."** Ahmad uses the relocation of Delhi's Idgah abattoir to Ghazipur to show how urban modernization displaces marginal workers. Mechanization and relocation did not simply improve hygiene. They reorganized an occupational network, excluded workers, and shifted hazards to the city's edge.

**Jonathan Parry, "Suicide in a Central Indian Steel Town."** Parry connects suicide around Bhilai to the downsizing of public-sector employment after liberalization. The central problem is a crisis in reproducing the secure class position of permanent workers, compounded by the way families, police, and official statistics obscure motives.
