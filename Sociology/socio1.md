# Introduction to sociology: agency and structure

> [!summary]
> Sociology asks how much room people have to act inside arrangements they did not choose. **Agency** names the capacity to act; **structure** names the institutions and unequal power relations that shape the available choices. Family, caste, class, gender, religion, the state, and the market all matter here.

## What is sociology?

Sociology systematically studies society, social relationships, institutions, and interaction. Here, the focus is the tension between:

- The **individual or self**
- **Family and friends**
- **Communities and groups**
- **Society as a whole**

Unlike psychology, which often begins with the individual mind, sociology asks how wider relationships and institutions shape what individuals feel, believe, choose, and become.

> [!important]
> Sociology does not treat the individual and society as separate. It studies how each is formed through its relationship with the other.

## Agency and structure

### Agency

**Agency** is an individual's capacity to act, make choices, and influence their surroundings within a particular time and place.

Examples include:

- Choosing a college or degree
- Deciding how to teach a class
- Participating in or rejecting competition
- Selecting friends or partners
- Protesting or remaining inactive

Agency does not mean unlimited freedom. People choose from options made available to them, and those options are not distributed equally.

### Structure

**Structure** refers to lasting social arrangements that enable, restrict, and organize action. Structures include institutions, norms, hierarchies, rules, and established relationships of power.

Examples include:

- An educational institution and its degree requirements
- The curriculum and examination system
- Family expectations
- Caste and class hierarchy
- Gender norms
- Religion
- The state and law
- Markets and privatization

### Their relationship

Agency and structure are not mutually exclusive explanations.

- Structures define the range and consequences of possible choices.
- Individuals interpret, reproduce, negotiate, or resist those structures.
- Repeated individual action can reinforce a structure.
- Collective action can gradually transform a structure.

For example, a teacher may personally choose a teaching method, but workload, curriculum, institutional rules, and available resources constrain that choice. The outcome is both individual and structural.

| Agency asks | Structure asks |
|---|---|
| What can this person choose or do? | What options and constraints already exist? |
| How does the individual respond? | Which institutions shape that response? |
| Can the person resist prevailing expectations? | What are the costs of resistance? |
| How does action change the surroundings? | How do power relations reproduce the surroundings? |

## Sociology and social problems

Sociology is often described as a discipline that helps solve social problems. Before solving one, however, sociologists must ask:

1. How is the problem defined?
2. Who experiences it and in what ways?
3. Which institutions produce or maintain it?
4. Which parts involve individual action?
5. How is it connected to other problems?
6. Whose interests are served by the status quo?

The lecture uses the Indian education system to demonstrate this method.

## Education as a social system

Problems identified in the education system included:

- Outdated or narrowly utilitarian curricula
- Memorization and insufficient hands-on learning
- Hierarchy between STEM, arts, and other disciplines
- Hierarchy among examinations and institutions
- Rural-urban inequality
- Unequal access to quality institutions and limited seats
- Privatization
- Infrastructure shortages
- Regional differences in policy implementation
- Policy paralysis
- Uneven quality of teaching
- Overworked students and educators
- Excessive competition
- Corruption, fraud, and unfair evaluation
- Inequality based on caste, class, gender, and religion
- Mental-health pressures

These issues are not isolated. Each is connected to other institutions and power relations.

### Structural, individual, or both?

| Issue | Structural dimension | Individual dimension |
|---|---|---|
| Curriculum | Institutions prescribe content and priorities | Teachers interpret and deliver it differently |
| Teaching quality | Workload, hiring, training, and resources shape performance | Educators make choices within those conditions |
| Competition | Scarce seats and rank-based evaluation encourage comparison | Individuals may embrace or resist competitive behavior |
| Subject hierarchy | Labor markets and institutions reward some disciplines more | Families and students reproduce those rankings |
| Caste discrimination | Historical hierarchy and institutional arrangements persist | Individuals enact, normalize, or challenge prejudice |
| Gender discrimination | Law, organizations, and cultural norms distribute opportunity unequally | Individuals may reproduce bias despite recognizing it |
| Mental health | Academic pressure and inequality shape everyday well-being | Personal conditions and coping also matter |

> [!note]
> Labeling a problem "structural" does not mean individuals have no agency. It means their choices occur under pressures and unequal conditions that cannot be explained by personality alone.

## Competition and merit

The lecture distinguishes **working toward a goal** from **competition**.

- Goal-oriented effort: "If I work hard, I may improve my outcome."
- Competitive reasoning: "My success depends on someone else receiving a worse outcome."

An educational system based on relative rank can make another person's loss seem necessary for one's own success. Students may oppose a fair adjustment for others even when their own marks are unaffected because status is comparative.

This illustrates how:

1. A structure creates incentives for competition.
2. Individuals internalize those incentives.
3. Their behavior then reproduces the competitive structure.

The expression **"चार लोग क्या कहेंगे"** (*What will people say?*) captures the influence of an imagined audience. Social comparison can shape choices even when no specific observer directly commands the individual.

## Hierarchy of disciplines

The preference for STEM over arts, humanities, and social sciences is both structural and social.

### Structural sources

- Employment opportunities appear more direct or visible in STEM.
- Institutions and markets assign different rewards and prestige to disciplines.
- Unequal exposure means some groups have less information about non-STEM careers.

### Social and individual reproduction

- Families encourage subjects associated with secure employment or status.
- Gender norms may frame science and computing as masculine.
- Students rank institutions and disciplines through competitive comparison.
- Class and caste shape which educational risks a family can afford.

The same course of study can therefore carry different meanings in different social locations. What appears to be free choice may reflect prior socialization and unequal access to alternatives.

## Institutions and relations of power

The lecture's central proposition is:

> **Social structure is composed of relations of power among institutions.**

An **institution** is not only a formal organization. It can be a durable system of norms, roles, and relationships.

Institutions discussed include:

- Family
- Education
- Caste
- Class
- Gender
- Religion
- State
- Market
- Media and digital platforms

These institutions interact rather than operate independently. For example, educational access may be shaped simultaneously by:

- Family income
- Caste position
- Gender expectations
- State policy
- Market-based privatization
- Regional infrastructure

Power lies in the capacity of these relationships to enable some actions, identities, and futures while disabling others.

## The Jenga model of society

The classroom used a Jenga tower to model the education system.

- Each block represented a social problem or institution.
- The whole tower represented the functioning education system.
- Removing a block represented solving a problem.
- The tower's instability represented unintended effects on the wider system.
- Refusing to remove blocks preserved the **status quo**.

### Lessons from the model

1. **Problems are interdependent:** changing one element affects others.
2. **Reform is difficult:** a simple solution can destabilize connected arrangements.
3. **The status quo still functions:** preserving it avoids immediate collapse but also preserves injustice.
4. **Change takes time:** persistent problems are not evidence that nobody has tried to solve them.
5. **No isolated fix is sufficient:** reforms must account for relations among institutions.
6. **An ideal system cannot be created by subtraction alone:** institutions and relationships may need reconstruction, not merely removal.

> [!warning]
> A proposal that claims one action will completely solve a long-standing social problem ignores structural interdependence.

### Limits of the analogy

The tower suggests a fixed structure, but actual society is dynamic. People can redesign institutions, build alternatives, change power relations, and collectively create new possibilities. Reform need not only mean carefully removing existing pieces.

## Choice and unequal opportunity

Structures present people with sets of options, but the number and quality of those options differ.

- One person may have many educational and career paths.
- Another may have only one viable path because of poverty, family responsibility, location, or discrimination.
- A formal right to choose does not guarantee a meaningful range of choices.

### Example: economic constraint

A child whose earning parent dies may have to leave school and become a migrant worker. This outcome involves action, but calling it a free choice conceals the absence of realistic alternatives.

### Example: gender and education during COVID-19

When colleges closed, some women were expected to perform unpaid household labor or marry rather than continue education. Their altered educational trajectories emerged from the interaction of:

- Family
- Gender norms
- State responses
- Labor and education markets
- Economic pressure

Agency remains present, but its exercise has unequal costs.

## Lifeworld

The **lifeworld** is the taken-for-granted world of everyday meaning through which a person understands themselves, others, and what appears possible.

It includes:

- Sense of self
- Values and beliefs
- Ideas of respect and obligation
- Relationships with family and community
- Friends and social networks
- Aspirations and imagined futures
- Ordinary habits and communication

Structures shape the available choices and what people learn to desire. A student may come to want engineering because family, peers, markets, status hierarchies, and institutions have made that path seem natural or desirable.

> [!important]
> Structure does not merely block action from outside. It helps form the self that acts.

## Socialization

**Socialization** is the process through which people learn norms, roles, identities, expectations, and ways of interpreting the world.

Sources of socialization include:

- Family
- School
- Peer groups
- Religion
- Media
- Social class
- Caste community
- Professional and educational institutions

Socialization explains why the same option can appear respectable and realistic to one group but risky or unacceptable to another.

## Ascribed and achieved status

Sociology distinguishes two forms of status.

### Ascribed status

An **ascribed status** is assigned without being chosen, often at birth.

Examples include:

- Family of origin
- Caste position
- Inherited class location
- Birthplace
- Social identities assigned by others

### Achieved status

An **achieved status** is acquired through action, performance, or later life processes.

Examples include:

- Educational qualification
- Occupation
- Institutional membership
- Some relationships and social roles

### The connection

The distinction is analytical rather than absolute. Ascribed status affects the resources, recognition, and opportunities available for attaining achieved status.

For example:

- Family wealth affects access to schooling and coaching.
- Caste and gender can affect treatment within institutions.
- Social networks affect information and opportunity.
- Family expectations influence educational or marital choices.

Thus, even achievements attributed to individual merit are socially conditioned.

## Caste beyond reservation

The lecture challenges the argument that reservation itself creates or sustains caste discrimination.

Removing reservation would not automatically eliminate caste because caste operates across many social settings, not only educational admissions. Prejudice may persist in:

- Family expectations
- Friendship and social networks
- Housing
- Employment
- Dating and marriage
- Everyday interaction

### Dating-app example

Digital dating platforms reduce limits of physical proximity and make initial communication easier. Yet caste preferences can reappear when users seek long-term relationships or marriage.

This demonstrates that:

- New technology can change how people meet without eliminating old hierarchies.
- Apparently personal romantic choices can reproduce social boundaries.
- The platform's affordances enable agency, while family and caste structures continue to shape its use.

## Gender as structure and practice

Gender is not limited to a male-female binary; it includes a broader spectrum of identities. Gender inequality is structural because it is embedded in:

- Laws and state procedures
- Institutional practices
- Occupational segregation
- Family expectations
- Cultural stereotypes

It is also reproduced by individuals who continue discriminatory behavior, including those who understand the inequality involved.

### Computing and masculinity

The belief that computing is naturally masculine is socially produced rather than evidence of innate ability.

- Unequal encouragement affects who enters the field.
- Hiring and workplace practices reproduce gender imbalance.
- Historical cultures around technology shape who appears to belong.
- Existing imbalance is then misread as proof of natural difference.

This is a circular process: structure shapes participation, and unequal participation is used to justify the structure.

## Mental health: sociological perspective

A purely individual explanation may frame mental health as a matter of personality, diagnosis, meditation, or personal coping. Sociology asks how everyday well-being is also shaped by:

- Competition
- Workload
- Evaluation systems
- Economic insecurity
- Discrimination
- Family pressure
- Social isolation
- Institutional support or neglect

Individual mental conditions remain real, but they do not capture the whole social distribution of distress.

| Individual lens | Sociological lens |
|---|---|
| What condition or coping pattern does this person have? | Which social conditions repeatedly produce distress? |
| How can this person adapt? | Why are particular groups exposed to greater pressure? |
| What treatment helps the individual? | What institutional changes could reduce harm? |

The two lenses can complement one another. Sociology rejects reducing a structurally produced condition to personal failure.

## Imagined communities and digital communication

Social media allows people who never meet to share language, symbols, identities, and political demands. Such a group can form an **imagined community**: members experience connection despite not knowing most other members personally.

### Shared language

Generation-specific slang, gestures, memes, images, and political references can:

- Mark membership in a community
- Carry ideas rapidly across regions
- Turn local grievances into shared demands
- Connect present action to historical symbols
- Coordinate protest and collective identity

Language is therefore not merely a tool for describing a movement. It can help create the movement's community.

### Political symbols and memory

When a new generation uses figures or symbols from past movements, historical discourse has not disappeared. It is being reinterpreted for a new context. Collective action draws on both new digital forms and inherited political memory.

## Globalization

**Globalization** is the cross-border flow of:

- People
- Ideas
- Images
- Information
- Cultural practices
- Goods and technologies

Online communication allows protest language, strategies, and solidarity to move across national boundaries. A community formed around a local issue may therefore become transnational.

These flows are not frictionless. States, platforms, language, censorship, inequality, and political conflict can help or obstruct them.

## Misinformation and disinformation

Digital communication can transmit harmful content as well as solidarity and knowledge.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Misinformation** | False or inaccurate information shared without deliberate intent to deceive |
| **Disinformation** | False or manipulated information deliberately created or spread to deceive |
| **Malinformation** | Genuine information used selectively or out of context to cause harm or mislead |

Repeatedly amplifying an isolated event can create a misleading picture of an entire religious, caste, or gender group even when the original event occurred. Sociological analysis asks:

- Who creates or amplifies the content?
- What identity or conflict does it construct?
- Which platform features increase its reach?
- Which groups are harmed?
- Who is accountable?

The spread of anti-Rohingya content through Facebook in Myanmar illustrates how platform communication can contribute to severe offline violence.

## Platform affordances and governance

An **affordance** is an action that a technology's design makes possible or easier.

Examples include:

- Dating apps reducing the importance of physical proximity
- Messaging reducing the awkwardness of initial contact
- Social media enabling rapid cross-regional communication
- Generative tools making synthetic or abusive content easier to produce

Technology does not determine a single outcome. The same affordance can be used differently within different social structures.

### Design and responsibility

When a platform knowingly supplies a feature that predictably enables abuse, responsibility cannot be placed only on individual users. Sociological analysis distributes responsibility across:

- User intentions
- Platform design
- Company incentives
- Moderation practices
- Law and regulation
- Wider cultural norms

This is the field of **platform governance**: how platforms create rules, enforce them, design technical possibilities, and become accountable for social consequences.

> [!note]
> "The user chose to do it" is an incomplete explanation when a platform designed, promoted, or profited from the capability that made the action easy and scalable.

## From self to society

The lecture's complete sociological chain is:

```text
Self <-> Family and friends <-> Community <-> Society
             ^                     ^
             |                     |
      institutions and relations of power
```

Sociology moves between these levels to understand:

- How structures shape individual lives
- How people reproduce or resist structures
- How institutions interact
- How local communities connect across borders
- How personal problems can have social causes

## Exam-ready synthesis

1. Sociology studies the relationship between individuals and larger social formations.
2. Agency is the capacity to act; structure is the network of institutions and power relations that enables and restricts action.
3. Social problems such as educational inequality are rarely purely individual or purely structural.
4. The Jenga model shows that institutions and problems are interdependent, making reform difficult and preservation of the status quo tempting.
5. Family, caste, class, gender, religion, state, market, and education interact to distribute opportunities unequally.
6. Structures shape both available choices and the lifeworld through which people form identities and aspirations.
7. Ascribed status affects the possibility of achieving later statuses, complicating simple ideas of merit.
8. Personal relationships, mental health, and technology use can all reproduce social structures.
9. Digital language creates imagined communities and carries collective action across borders through globalization.
10. Platform design and governance help determine whether digital affordances support community, manipulation, or harm.

## Key terms

- **Sociology:** Study of society, social relationships, institutions, and the relationship between individuals and groups.
- **Agency:** Capacity to act and make choices within social conditions.
- **Structure:** Lasting arrangement of institutions, norms, hierarchies, and power relations.
- **Institution:** Durable system of roles, norms, and relationships organizing social life.
- **Power relation:** Unequal capacity to enable, constrain, or shape another actor's options.
- **Status quo:** Existing social arrangement, especially when preserved rather than transformed.
- **Lifeworld:** Everyday world of taken-for-granted meanings, identities, relationships, and possibilities.
- **Socialization:** Process through which people learn social norms, roles, identities, and expectations.
- **Ascribed status:** Social position assigned without individual choice.
- **Achieved status:** Social position acquired through action or accomplishment.
- **Imagined community:** Community whose members feel connected without personally knowing most other members.
- **Globalization:** Cross-border flow of people, ideas, images, information, goods, and practices.
- **Affordance:** Action a technology's design makes possible or easier.
- **Platform governance:** Rules, designs, moderation, and accountability through which platforms organize user activity.
- **Misinformation:** Inaccurate information spread without intent to deceive.
- **Disinformation:** Inaccurate or manipulated information deliberately spread to deceive.
