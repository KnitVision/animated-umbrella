# Social stratification: class, caste, and life chances

> [!summary]
> **Social stratification** is the durable ranking of social groups, through which people receive unequal access to money, respect, power, safety, education, and health. The lecture contrasts **class**, where movement is possible but unequal, with **caste**, where rank is attached to birth and protected through rules of occupation, contact, and marriage. Marx explains class through exploitation and the reserve army of labour. Weber adds status and party, showing why money alone does not explain privilege. Ambedkar's sharpest objection is that caste is not merely a division of work. It is a division of workers, fixed to bodies and made hereditary.

## What makes a social difference into stratification?

Societies contain many differences. People have different jobs, languages, religions, interests, and skills. Difference becomes **stratification** when it is arranged vertically as a ranking and when that rank changes people's real opportunities.

| Horizontal difference | Vertical stratification |
|---|---|
| Different jobs have comparable rewards and respect. | Jobs and groups are ranked unequally. |
| A cleaner, teacher, engineer, and manager can differ without one life being systematically more secure. | Income, dignity, authority, housing, education, and security accumulate unequally. |
| Difference need not determine a child's future. | Rank tends to shape the next generation's future too. |

Giddens defines social stratification as structured inequality between social groups. It can rest on property, but also on gender, age, religion, race, ethnicity, caste, or military rank. The key point is unequal access to rewards, not mere variation between people.

### Life chances

**Life chances** are the opportunities a person has to live a healthy, secure, and self-directed life. They are not just a matter of effort or talent.

The lecture's comparison was simple. Two children may be born in the same city on the same day. One is born into a household with safe housing, money, nutrition, regular vaccination, good schools, transport, and family networks. The other is born in an informal settlement with insecure work, weak public services, and greater exposure to illness and eviction. They have not made different choices. The social ranking around them has already given them unequal starting points.

```text
Class, caste, gender, and location
                ↓
Access to food, care, schools, safety, networks, and money
                ↓
Different life chances
                ↓
Advantages and disadvantages carried into the next generation
```

This is why a sociological explanation cannot stop at "they should have worked harder." Personal effort matters, but it operates inside conditions a person did not choose.

### Capabilities, poverty, and justice

The lecture connects life chances to Amartya Sen's **capability approach**. Poverty is not simply low income or a lack of possessions. It is a lack of real freedom to do things one has reason to value: to be healthy, learn, travel safely, participate in public life, and pursue work without being trapped by deprivation.

Someone may technically have a school nearby or a formal right to compete for a job, yet lack the nutrition, time, transport, documents, language, money, safety, or social support needed to use that opportunity. Formal equality is therefore not the same as equal capability.

> [!important]
> The question for public policy is not only "Are opportunities formally open?" It is "Who has the material and social capacity to use them?"

Climate crisis makes this point harder to ignore. Poor people usually face heat, flood, disease, crop failure, and displacement first. But a city built on unequal infrastructure is not safe for the privileged either. Flooding in wealthy office districts shows that private capital cannot substitute for public planning, drainage, housing, and welfare. The damage is unequal, but it does not stay neatly contained within one class.

## Class: mobility within an unequal system

A **class** is a large group of people with similar economic resources and a similar position in the market or production process. Class boundaries are not legally fixed in the way caste boundaries have historically been. A person can move up or down, and people from different classes can marry. That openness is real, but it should not be exaggerated.

### Social mobility

**Social mobility** means movement between socioeconomic positions.

- **Upward mobility** means moving to a more advantaged position.
- **Downward mobility** means losing income, security, property, or status.
- **Intragenerational mobility** happens during one person's working life.
- **Intergenerational mobility** compares children with parents or grandparents.
- **Lateral mobility** means movement across place or occupation without necessarily moving up or down the hierarchy.

The lecture used *Gully Boy* to make the appeal of upward mobility visible. Murad's success as a rapper disrupts expectations attached to his class location. It is meaningful precisely because class mobility is imaginable.

But the exceptional success story can hide the structure. A few people rising does not show that everyone had an equal chance to rise. It may even make inequality look deserved by turning structural advantage into a story of individual hustle.

### Preserving class in a new economy

Mobility is not only a poor person moving upward. A family with resources may move from a declining family business into start-ups, professional education, finance, or technology in order to preserve its class position. This is often described as innovation or personal ambition, but it also depends on inherited capital, networks, safety nets, and the ability to take risks.

The "new economy" does not erase class. It gives advantaged families new ways to reproduce it.

## Marx: exploitation, pauperization, and the reserve army

For Marx, class is rooted in people's relation to the **means of production**. Capitalists own or control productive property. Workers must sell their **labour power** in order to live. Profit depends on paying workers less than the value their work creates, which Marx calls **exploitation**.

### Pauperization and the reserve army of labour

**Pauperization** refers to the production or deepening of poverty under capitalism. Marx's argument is not merely that some people will be poor. Capitalism continually produces a pool of people seeking work who can replace those already employed.

This pool is the **reserve army of labour**.

```text
Workers need wages to survive
             +
Unemployed and underemployed people need jobs
             ↓
Employers can threaten replacement
             ↓
Workers' bargaining power and wages are held down
```

The lecture applied this to engineering and tech. Large numbers of graduates entering the job market make individual workers more replaceable. This does not mean every graduate is identical or powerless. It means employers benefit when the supply of job-seekers is larger than the supply of secure jobs.

> [!note]
> A labour shortage can strengthen workers' bargaining power. A large reserve of job-seekers usually strengthens the employer's power to set wages and conditions.

This continues the discussion in [[socio10|Social reproduction: care, automation, and labor control]]. Automation, outsourcing, and informalisation can enlarge insecurity even when productivity rises.

## Caste: why mobility is not enough

Giddens describes caste as a form of stratification in which social position is ascribed at birth. It is a useful starting point, but the lecture pushed the analysis further through Ambedkar.

In a class system, unequal positions can be changed, at least in principle. In caste, a person's rank, expected occupation, access to social spaces, and marriage possibilities have historically been attached to birth. This makes caste a **closed** form of stratification.

### Varna and jati are not the same thing

The four-fold **varna** scheme groups society into Brahmins, Kshatriyas, Vaishyas, and Shudras. Dalits and many other communities do not fit neatly into this scheme and were historically placed outside it. The scheme should not be confused with the lived complexity of caste.

**Jati** refers to the many local, hereditary caste groups through which caste is actually lived. A jati's rank and meaning can vary by region. Saying someone is simply "Brahmin" or "Shudra" therefore tells us little about their specific social location without asking which community and where.

> [!warning]
> Varna is a classificatory model. It does not describe the full diversity of Indian caste society, and it has often been used to make hierarchy look natural or functional.

### The division of labourers

Ambedkar's distinction is central:

| Division of labour | Division of labourers |
|---|---|
| Tasks are distributed across people. | People themselves are sorted into ranked, hereditary groups. |
| A worker can in principle learn or move to another task. | Birth fixes the worker's expected occupation and social worth. |
| The task is divided. | The body and community are divided. |

Every complex society needs many kinds of work. That fact does not require a hereditary hierarchy. Caste turns an economic need for work into a system that guarantees a supply of particular workers, generation after generation, while making some work and some bodies socially degraded.

This is why calling caste a "functional division of labour" misses the point. The issue is not that different work exists. The issue is that people are denied the freedom to choose, leave, or be treated with equal dignity in that work.

### Jajmani and hereditary occupation

The lecture used the **jajmani system** to explain how caste occupation could be reproduced in village life. A *jajman* was a patron or dominant landholding household. Service-providing families supplied particular goods or labour in return for grain, payment, or customary claims.

Because these relationships could continue across generations, a family's work became tied to caste and birth. This produced dependence, not a free contract between equal parties.

### Purity, pollution, and social distance

Louis Dumont's book-centred account treats purity and pollution as the organizing idea of caste. The lecture's point was not that this account is sufficient. It helps explain why hierarchy enters ordinary practices of proximity, food, water, touch, and space.

**Social intercourse** means everyday social mixing: eating together, sharing water, visiting homes, travelling together, speaking as equals, and forming intimate relationships. Caste has historically restricted these acts because unrestricted mixing weakens the boundary between ranked groups.

The distinction between *kaccha* and *pakka* food illustrates this. Rules about who may cook for whom, and whose food or water can be accepted, appear as custom. Sociologically, they regulate intimacy and reproduce hierarchy.

The lecture gave a contemporary example: sanitation workers who do not use the same lift as residents, even without a written prohibition. Sometimes someone has explicitly excluded them. Often people anticipate exclusion and keep themselves apart. The system can work through habit, embarrassment, and silent expectation as well as direct orders.

### Endogamy

**Endogamy** means marriage within one's own social group. For Ambedkar, caste survives above all through endogamy because it reproduces the group across generations.

The preference for a caste-matched partner can be presented as compatibility, family convenience, or a way to avoid conflict. These explanations may be sincere. They also preserve caste boundaries by limiting who can form a household, inherit property, claim kinship, and pass status to children.

Dating apps do not automatically dissolve this system. A platform may widen the pool of possible partners, while family approval, caste filters, surnames, and ideas of "matching background" still narrow the choice when a relationship becomes serious.

> [!important]
> Caste does not persist only because somebody loudly announces a caste rule. It also persists when ordinary choices repeatedly follow caste boundaries.

## Class and caste together

Class and caste overlap, but they are not interchangeable.

- Class concerns resources, property, work, market position, and economic mobility.
- Caste concerns hereditary rank, social honour, stigma, marriage, and restrictions on intimacy and occupation.
- A person may gain income or education without escaping caste discrimination.
- A caste group may contain people with different class positions.

This is why reservation cannot be dismissed as a policy that merely "keeps caste alive." The lecture's argument was that naming caste in policy does not create the hierarchy. It responds to a hierarchy already shaping access to education, employment, dignity, and political power. Economic redistribution by itself cannot remove caste if endogamy, stigma, and exclusion from social life remain intact.

The difficult question is whether caste would disappear if the state stopped recognizing it. The class discussion suggests the opposite answer: ignoring an existing structure generally leaves its accumulated advantages and disadvantages in place.

## Representation, transgression, and the rescue narrative

*Gully Boy* shows a working-class Muslim protagonist moving upward and crossing religious boundaries in a shared neighbourhood. The film matters because it makes solidarity and mobility imaginable.

Yet popular culture more readily tells a class-mobility story than a caste-transgression story. Caste reaches into family, marriage, everyday respect, and the body itself. It is harder to represent as an obstacle that one talented individual can simply outwork.

The lecture also discussed *Article 15*. Its upper-caste police officer acts against caste violence, but the story risks making the privileged outsider the person who sees, speaks, and rescues. A sympathetic ally can matter. The sociological question is whether representation centres Dalit agency or turns justice into proof of an upper-caste hero's goodness.

Representation has limits. A brown Barbie or a more diverse screen can challenge who is visible, but it does not by itself change property, work, caste, school access, or marriage boundaries. Recognition matters. Redistribution and power matter too.

## Weber: class, status, and party

[[Max Weber]] agrees with Marx that capitalism produces inequality, but he does not reduce stratification to ownership alone. He identifies three related dimensions.

| Dimension | What it means | Lecture example |
|---|---|---|
| **Class** | Similar resources and market situation | Money, education, credentials, contacts, and employability shape life chances. |
| **Status** | Social honour, prestige, and style of life | Two people may have similar wealth, yet an IAS officer receives a distinctive respect and authority. |
| **Party** | Organized power used to influence collective decisions | Groups form organizations and political alliances to pursue interests. |

For Weber, **market situation** matters. Credentials, skills, education, reputation, networks, and property affect what a person can sell in the market and on what terms. This is more expansive than treating class as income alone.

```text
Economic resources and market position  -> class
Social honour and recognition           -> status
Organized influence over decisions      -> party
```

The three dimensions overlap without being identical. Money can buy status signals, but it does not automatically confer the authority of a prestigious office. A high-status group can exercise power through political organization even where its members differ in wealth.

This connects back to [[socio9|Max Weber: social action and rationalities]]. Weber's broader point is that culture, law, honour, and organization do not merely reflect the economy. They also help reproduce unequal social positions.

## Exam-ready synthesis

1. Social stratification is a durable system of unequal ranking, not simply social difference.
2. Stratification shapes life chances before an individual makes choices.
3. Sen's capability approach asks whether people have real freedom to use opportunities, not merely formal rights.
4. Class systems permit some mobility, but inherited resources make mobility unequal.
5. Marx explains class through ownership, exploitation, and workers' dependence on selling labour power.
6. The reserve army of labour makes workers more replaceable and weakens their bargaining power.
7. Caste is an ascribed and historically closed system that ties rank to birth.
8. Varna is not the same as jati. Jati names the many locally organized caste groups through which caste is lived.
9. Ambedkar distinguishes a division of labour from a division of labourers. Caste fixes people, not only tasks.
10. Jajmani, purity-pollution rules, restricted social intercourse, and endogamy help reproduce caste.
11. Caste and class intersect, but money alone cannot abolish caste stigma or endogamy.
12. Weber explains stratification through class, status, and party. Resources, honour, and organized power all shape life chances.

## Key terms

- **Social stratification:** Structured inequality between social groups.
- **Life chances:** Socially shaped opportunities for health, security, education, and self-directed action.
- **Capability:** Real freedom to achieve valued ways of being and doing.
- **Class:** A large group sharing a similar economic and market position.
- **Social mobility:** Movement between socioeconomic positions.
- **Pauperization:** Production or worsening of poverty under capitalism.
- **Reserve army of labour:** Unemployed or underemployed people whose availability makes employed workers more replaceable.
- **Caste:** Hereditary social hierarchy tied to birth, rank, occupation, and social relations.
- **Varna:** Four-fold idealized hierarchy of Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, and Shudra, which does not capture the full caste system.
- **Jati:** A locally specific hereditary caste group.
- **Jajmani system:** Hereditary patron-service relations that tied families to customary occupations.
- **Endogamy:** Marriage within one's own group.
- **Social intercourse:** Everyday interaction and intimacy, including eating, sharing water, visiting, and marriage.
- **Status:** Social honour or prestige that marks people even within the same class.
- **Party:** Organized group power directed toward collective decisions.
