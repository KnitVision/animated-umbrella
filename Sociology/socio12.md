# Caste in contemporary India: resources, representation, and annihilation

> [!summary]
> Caste persists through the unequal distribution of work, land, education, networks, dignity, and political power. It does not survive only as untouchability or an old religious rule. It also works through hiring, promotion, marriage, language, informal expectations, and violence against people who cross symbolic boundaries. Representation and reservation can open institutions, but neither automatically changes the structure that produces exclusion. An anti-caste politics must therefore join material redistribution to changes in social relations, while taking seriously the history that makes solidarity difficult.

## Caste-linked work has not disappeared

The lecture began with occupations that remain heavily concentrated among Dalit communities. These include sanitation work, cremation work, sewer and drain cleaning, removal of dead animals, and other forms of labour treated as dirty or polluting. Mining, construction, and agricultural wage labour are less exclusive, but Dalit workers are still overrepresented in them.

This pattern cannot be explained as a free occupational choice. It reflects unequal control over resources.

```text
Little or no land
        +
Weak access to education, credit, and secure employment
        ↓
Dependence on daily-wage and stigmatized work
        ↓
Low income, poor health, and weak bargaining power
        ↓
Disadvantage reproduced in the next generation
```

A household with enough cultivable land can remain in the village or choose when to migrate. A landless household often cannot. Construction workers in cities may therefore appear to have left a caste occupation while still carrying the effects of caste-shaped landlessness.

> [!important]
> Occupational change does not prove that caste has vanished. The better question is who owns productive resources, who performs insecure labour, and who can refuse degrading work.

This develops Ambedkar's distinction in [[socio11|Social stratification: class, caste, and life chances]]. Caste is not merely a division of tasks. It is a division of workers that gives some communities a near monopoly over desirable work and traps others in work that everyone needs but few will share.

## Life chances and the myth of deficient merit

When a group remains poor, a common explanation is that its members lack ability, ambition, or merit. The lecture rejects this as a reductionist account of human beings. Ability develops through nutrition, health, safety, schooling, language, time, and social encouragement.

The example was a child in a flood-prone village in Assam who must swim across a river with her school bag sealed in plastic. If she later performs poorly in an examination, the result cannot be read as a pure measure of intelligence. The examination records the effects of the route to school, missed classes, danger, hunger, and weak infrastructure as well as what she knows.

This is the force of Amartya Sen's **capability approach**. Poverty is not only a shortage of money. It is the absence of real opportunities to be educated, healthy, mobile, secure, and able to participate in public life.

| Formal equality | Substantive capability |
|---|---|
| Everyone may sit the same examination. | People need comparable nutrition, teaching, time, language access, and safety to compete. |
| A school formally exists. | A child must be able to reach and attend it regularly. |
| Anyone may apply for a job. | Applicants need credentials, networks, confidence, and freedom from discrimination. |
| The law permits mobility. | People need resources and social acceptance to make mobility durable. |

Merit is real, but it is produced socially. Treating an outcome as proof of natural ability erases the conditions that made it possible.

## Graded inequality

Ambedkar describes caste as a system of **graded inequality**. Society is not divided into only two camps, such as rich and poor or oppressor and oppressed. It is arranged in a ladder. Each rank may be subordinate to someone above it while retaining an advantage over someone below it.

```text
Rank above
   ↑ aspiration and deference
Middle rank
   ↓ distance and domination
Rank below
```

This structure weakens solidarity. A group may oppose domination from above while defending its own status against another group. The system offers small privileges to many ranks, which gives them a reason to preserve the ladder even when they suffer within it.

### Caste and class as conjugated oppression

Caste and class should not be collapsed into one another. They work together.

- Class shapes ownership, income, employment security, and control over productive resources.
- Caste shapes inherited rank, occupational concentration, marriage, stigma, and social networks.
- Landlessness can force a person into wage labour.
- Caste can determine which wage labour is available and how the worker is treated.
- Economic mobility can improve a person's options without removing caste stigma.

The result is **conjugated oppression**, in which economic exploitation and caste hierarchy reinforce each other. A Dalit entrepreneur, officer, or student may gain class mobility while still facing questions about surname, admission rank, marriage, or presumed competence.

## Opulence is also a display of power

The lecture used the Ambani wedding to distinguish consumption from power. A spectacular wedding certainly displays wealth, but it also gathers business leaders, political figures, celebrities, and state officials in one visible network.

The display matters even to someone who was never invited. A local official deciding whether to approve a project has already seen the corporation's proximity to national power. Public spectacle can therefore communicate influence before anyone issues a direct instruction.

```text
Private wealth
     ↓
Access to political and corporate elites
     ↓
Public display of those relationships
     ↓
Officials and citizens anticipate influence
```

The point is not that every official decision follows from one wedding. It is that networks, visibility, and anticipated consequences form part of power.

## Caste regulates who may display status

The same display does not carry the same meaning for every caste. Practices associated with dignity or prosperity can become dangerous when Dalits perform them.

The lecture referred to violence over acts such as:

- A Dalit groom riding a horse in his wedding procession
- A family cooking a wedding feast with ghee
- A Dalit man keeping a long or prominent moustache

None of these acts harms another person. They become punishable only within a social order that treats certain symbols of honour as the property of dominant castes.

> [!note]
> Boundary violence is not a reaction to material injury. It disciplines a claim to equality.

This is why caste violence can increase when marginalized people become more visible or mobile. The violence is not evidence that the old boundary remains untouched. It may be a reaction to the boundary being crossed.

## Caste in the modern economy

Contemporary caste often works without an explicit rule. A job may be formally open and an applicant may enter through merit, yet treatment inside the organization can depend on social networks and unspoken judgments.

### Social and cultural capital

A person raised in a business family begins a start-up with knowledge, contacts, credit, and a safety net. A person raised around the film industry learns how to behave before a camera and whom to approach for an audition. These advantages do not prove that the person lacks talent. They show that talent develops and circulates through family environments.

The same logic applies to caste networks:

- Information about jobs and contracts travels through trusted circles.
- Interviewers may feel greater familiarity with candidates from similar backgrounds.
- Mentors and senior colleagues influence promotion.
- Family wealth makes failure survivable.
- Accent, clothing, confidence, and English fluency are read as signs of competence.

English is especially double-edged. It can reproduce privilege because access to fluent English is unequal. It can also offer marginalized people a route out of local caste relations and into wider labour markets. Individual escape through English or migration is meaningful, but it does not amount to collective annihilation of caste.

### Explicit and implicit discrimination

Social structure does not need to appear as a written instruction. People learn its categories and carry them into decisions.

| Explicit caste practice | Implicit caste practice |
|---|---|
| A caste slur | Treating an accent as proof of low ability |
| Refusing entry or service | Choosing a familiar surname without naming caste |
| Asking directly about caste | Asking an admission rank to infer reservation status |
| Rejecting an inter-caste marriage | Describing a caste-matched partner as a better cultural fit |

This is one reason caste can persist inside modern universities, firms, and bureaucracies. Formal rules may prohibit discrimination while ordinary judgments continue to reproduce it.

## Identity politics and the limits of inclusion

**Identity politics** organizes claims around a shared social identity, such as caste, race, religion, gender, or sexuality. Such organization can make excluded people visible and create political representation. The lecture's criticism is narrower: inclusion can stop at accommodation and leave the hierarchy intact.

Corporate Pride campaigns offer a clear analogy. A company can display a rainbow flag while queer employees remain unsafe, closeted, underrepresented in leadership, or vulnerable to discrimination. The symbol is not worthless, but it cannot substitute for changes in hiring, benefits, workplace culture, and authority.

The same distinction applies to caste and **diversity, equity, and inclusion**, or DEI.

| Inclusion | Structural change |
|---|---|
| A marginalized person enters an institution. | Rules, resources, and power inside the institution change. |
| A party gives a candidate a ticket. | The representative can challenge the party's caste interests. |
| A company celebrates diversity. | Discrimination in recruitment, pay, promotion, and everyday treatment declines. |
| A group receives a separate space. | Segregation and the hierarchy behind it are dismantled. |

Representation matters because absence also reproduces power. The mistake is to treat presence as the end of the struggle.

### Vote banks and constrained representation

Caste offers political parties a ready basis for mobilization. Parties can assemble vote banks, distribute tickets, and promise a share of state resources without attacking caste hierarchy itself.

A representative from a marginalized caste may hold office but remain dependent on the party that awarded the seat. This does not make representation fake. It means office, voice, and autonomy are different forms of power.

The Mandal era expanded the political visibility of Other Backward Classes and changed who could claim state power. It also showed that mobilizing caste groups for representation does not automatically produce the annihilation of caste. Electoral arithmetic can make caste identities more politically useful even when a movement speaks in the language of equality.

## Individual mobility is not collective emancipation

Capitalism can sometimes provide an exit from a local caste order. English education, professional work, migration, and global labour markets may allow a person to avoid forms of dependence that structure village life.

That escape should not be dismissed. But it remains partial:

- It is available to relatively few people.
- It can require leaving one's community rather than changing its conditions.
- Discrimination can follow the person into the new institution.
- Family and marriage may reactivate caste boundaries.
- The stigmatized occupation remains for someone else to perform.

The difference is between **mobility within a hierarchy** and **changing the hierarchy**.

## Endogamy survives liberal lifestyles

Education, wealth, professional employment, atheism, and liberal cultural tastes do not necessarily end caste. A person may date without asking about caste and still accept a caste-filtered arranged marriage when the family takes over the search.

This is not a minor inconsistency. Endogamy reproduces caste by controlling kinship, inheritance, property, and the social identity of children. A family can reject ritual conservatism in most of life while protecting caste at the point of marriage.

Class privilege can also make someone appear free of caste. Elite spaces reward English fluency and liberal manners, so direct caste or religious remarks may be considered impolite. The silence can reflect etiquette rather than structural equality.

## Varna, jati, and hereditary occupation

The lecture returned to the difference between **varna** and **jati**.

- **Varna** is the idealized four-fold scheme of Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, and Shudra, with Dalits placed outside the order.
- **Jati** is the lived, hereditary group through which occupation, marriage, rank, and local social relations are organized.

The varna story maps rank onto the body of a deity. Groups associated with the head are placed above those associated with the feet. The image turns hierarchy into a sacred and bodily order.

Jati operationalizes hierarchy. It assigns communities to occupations, regulates marriage, and reproduces the division of labourers by birth. A society needs teachers, cultivators, traders, cleaners, and craftspeople. It does not need to make those tasks hereditary or rank the people who perform them.

> [!important]
> The sociological problem is not specialization. It is inherited monopoly, forced occupation, and unequal dignity.

This is also the limit of a reform aimed only at untouchability. If stigmatized work, occupational monopoly, endogamy, and material privilege remain, a moral appeal for kinder behaviour leaves the machinery of caste in place.

## Solidarity carries the weight of history

Calls for all oppressed groups to unite begin from a real need. Class, caste, gender, religion, and region often overlap, and broad coalitions can confront forms of power that no group can change alone.

Solidarity cannot begin at year zero. Communities remember land conflict, humiliation, sexual violence, and massacres. A person whose family remembers violence by another community cannot simply be ordered to forget it for a common movement.

The lecture described Dalit agricultural labourers being burned after a wage struggle in Tamil Nadu. The details match the **Kilvenmani massacre of 1968**, not the Khairlanji killings mentioned in the transcription. The example shows why shared language or class location does not erase caste antagonism.

### Moments of transcendence

History does produce moments in which people act beyond established identities. The anti-CAA and anti-NRC protest at Shaheen Bagh brought Muslim women into public political life and drew support across communities. Women who had been described mainly through domestic roles appeared as speakers, organizers, and citizens.

Such moments matter because collective action can change what people think they are capable of doing. They remain openings, not guarantees. When the protest ends, household authority, party structures, and social divisions can return.

```text
Shared crisis or demand
          ↓
People act together across established boundaries
          ↓
New political capacities become visible
          ↓
Either organization deepens, or older relations reassert themselves
```

## Reservation after social mobility

The lecture rejected the simple choice between keeping reservation unchanged forever and abolishing it immediately. One proposal discussed was to connect benefits more closely to families, so that groups or households with several generations of mobility do not monopolize access.

The proposal addresses a real concern about the unequal distribution of benefits within reserved categories. It does not resolve the whole problem.

Economic and occupational mobility do not guarantee freedom from caste:

- Students may be asked their entrance rank to identify their admission category.
- A successful professional may still face caste slurs.
- An inter-caste relationship may still provoke family opposition or violence.
- Networks and inherited property remain unequally distributed.
- Private-sector discrimination is not repaired by mobility in government employment.

Reservation addresses access and representation in specified institutions. It cannot by itself abolish endogamy, stigma, residential segregation, or violence. Reform therefore requires evidence about who receives benefits and a clear account of which injury the policy is meant to repair.

## Why count caste?

Critics argue that a caste census may harden caste identities. The lecture answers that refusing to count caste does not erase its effects. Without data, the state cannot know how land, education, occupations, wealth, or public employment are distributed among caste groups.

The colonial census counted communities as part of a project of rule, classification, and division. A contemporary caste census must be judged by a different question: will the data reveal inequality and support accountable redistribution, or will it merely feed electoral competition?

| Colonial enumeration | Democratic enumeration |
|---|---|
| Classified populations to govern them. | Can measure unequal access to resources and institutions. |
| Treated communities as fixed administrative types. | Must allow complexity, self-identification, and scrutiny of categories. |
| Served an imperial state. | Should support public reasoning, policy evaluation, and claims for justice. |

Counting is a tool. Its political purpose and institutional safeguards determine what it does.

## The policy-practice gap

Well-designed policy does not travel directly from the state to an isolated citizen. Local officials, contractors, dominant groups, brokers, and family relations shape implementation.

### MGNREGA and direct benefit transfers

MGNREGA creates a legal guarantee of wage employment, while direct benefit transfer sends money to a worker's bank account to reduce leakage through middlemen. Yet an intermediary can return in another form. A contractor or local broker may enroll workers, transport them to an office, keep their ATM cards, or control access to payment.

```text
Formal entitlement
        ↓
Administrative system
        ↓
Local intermediaries and power relations
        ↓
Actual access to work and wages
```

Removing one official middleman does not remove the need for help with forms, transport, digital systems, or negotiations. If policy assumes that every citizen acts alone, informal power fills the gap.

### Infrastructure and caste hostility

The lecture also described electricity intended for a Dalit settlement being diverted by neighbouring villagers who did not need the power. The act makes little economic sense if policy is understood only as resource delivery. It makes sociological sense when equality itself threatens a status boundary.

This is the central policy lesson. A transformer, bank transfer, reserved seat, or legal right enters an existing social structure. Implementation can be blocked because people lose money, authority, or the satisfaction of keeping another group below them.

> [!warning]
> Policy design that ignores local social power will overestimate what formal access can achieve.

## Annihilation rather than accommodation

Ambedkar's demand for the **annihilation of caste** goes beyond adding marginalized people to existing institutions. It asks how hereditary rank, endogamy, occupational closure, and graded inequality can be dismantled.

No single measure is enough. The lecture points toward a connected programme:

- Redistribute access to land, education, credit, and secure work.
- Enforce protection against discrimination and caste violence.
- Expand representation without confusing it with full equality.
- Measure resource distribution through credible caste data.
- Reform reservation using evidence without reducing caste to income alone.
- Challenge endogamy and the everyday practices that reproduce social distance.
- Design policy around actual intermediaries and local power.
- Build solidarities that acknowledge historical violence instead of demanding amnesia.

The difficulty is political. People rarely surrender an advantage because they have been morally persuaded that hierarchy is wrong. Caste survives because it gives material, social, and psychological benefits to those who can use it.

## Exam-ready synthesis

1. Caste-linked occupations persist because hereditary stigma combines with unequal ownership of land, education, and capital.
2. Sen's capability approach shows why equal formal opportunity can coexist with radically unequal life chances.
3. Graded inequality creates a ladder in which groups can be dominated from above and dominant toward those below.
4. Caste and class are distinct but conjugated. Economic exploitation and inherited status reinforce each other.
5. Displays of wealth also display networks and political access.
6. Violence against Dalit wedding processions, food practices, or styles of dress enforces symbolic caste boundaries.
7. Modern caste works through social capital, language, hiring, promotion, and implicit judgments as well as direct exclusion.
8. Identity-based inclusion and representation matter, but they can accommodate people without changing the hierarchy.
9. Individual mobility through English, migration, or professional work does not equal collective emancipation.
10. Endogamy allows caste to survive inside otherwise liberal and educated families.
11. Varna supplies an ideology of rank, while jati organizes hereditary occupation and marriage in social life.
12. Solidarity must confront the memory of violence. Shared class or language does not automatically dissolve caste conflict.
13. Reservation improves access to specific institutions but cannot alone remove stigma, endogamy, or violence.
14. A caste census can reveal resource inequality, but counting must serve democratic accountability rather than administrative fixation.
15. Policy outcomes depend on local intermediaries and power. Formal entitlement is not the same as practical access.

## Key terms

- **Graded inequality:** A ranked hierarchy with several levels, where groups may be both subordinate and dominant.
- **Conjugated oppression:** The joint operation of caste, class, gender, or other structures in producing disadvantage.
- **Life chances:** Socially shaped opportunities for health, education, security, and self-directed action.
- **Capability:** A person's real freedom to achieve valued ways of living.
- **Social capital:** Resources gained through relationships, trust, and networks.
- **Cultural capital:** Skills, language, manners, and knowledge that institutions reward.
- **Identity politics:** Political organization and claims made through a shared social identity.
- **Tokenism:** Symbolic inclusion that leaves the distribution of power largely unchanged.
- **Endogamy:** Marriage within one's own social group.
- **Varna:** The idealized four-fold hierarchy used to explain caste rank.
- **Jati:** A lived hereditary community linked to marriage, occupation, and local status.
- **Reservation:** Constitutionally grounded measures that improve representation and access for historically disadvantaged groups.
- **Caste census:** Collection of caste data to measure population and the distribution of resources or opportunities.
- **Policy-practice gap:** The difference between a policy's formal design and its actual operation in social life.
- **Annihilation of caste:** Ambedkar's project of dismantling hereditary hierarchy, not merely softening its harshest practices.
