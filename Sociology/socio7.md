# Capitalist exploitation: labor power, aspiration, and consumption

> [!summary]
> Capitalism extracts surplus by extending and intensifying **labor power**, but modern workers may also discipline and overwork themselves. Production now stretches across global commodity chains, while a small group of hard-to-replace employees receives creative freedom, prestige, and shares. The wider system sustains itself through aspirations of upward mobility, consumer choice, and exceptional success stories. **Commodity fetishism** hides the labor and exploitation inside products, while digital platforms can even convert resistance to exploitation into profitable content.

## From exploitation to self-exploitation

The lecture begins with a scene from *Bugonia*. A CEO publicly promises an eight-hour workday and work-life balance, then tells employees to take unfinished work home. Management formally limits work while making workers personally responsible for meeting the same demands.

This produces **self-exploitation**. A worker does not need a supervisor standing nearby when deadlines, ambition, comparison, and fear of failure have become internal pressures.

The same pattern appears in academic life. Students and faculty may sacrifice sleep, relationships, and leisure to publish more papers or build stronger CVs than a job actually requires. The work looks voluntary, but institutional competition sets the standard.

```text
Formal freedom
      +
Competitive expectations
      +
Personal responsibility for outcomes
      ↓
Workers intensify their own labor
```

> [!important]
> Self-exploitation does not mean that the institution has stopped exerting power. It means that institutional demands now operate through the worker's own ambitions and habits.

This argument resembles Byung-Chul Han's account of the modern achievement subject. The worker becomes both exploiter and exploited by treating every limit as a personal failure to optimize.

## Labor power

**Labor power** is a person's capacity to work. It includes physical energy, skill, attention, knowledge, creativity, and endurance.

The lecture compared labor power with horsepower. James Watt used horsepower to express how many horses a steam engine could replace. The unit translated an animal's capacity into a measure useful for production. Capitalism similarly treats human capacity as something that can be measured, purchased, extended, and replaced.

### Labor power and the working day

An employer purchases labor power for a given period. If a worker can produce the value of their wage in part of the day, work performed beyond that point generates surplus for the owner.

```text
Working day = necessary labor time + surplus labor time
```

- **Necessary labor time** produces value equivalent to the worker's wage.
- **Surplus labor time** produces value retained by the owner as surplus.

Management can increase surplus by:

- Lengthening the working day
- Increasing the pace of work
- Raising productivity through machinery
- Moving work into unpaid personal time
- Reducing wages or benefits
- Shifting production to cheaper labor markets

The formal eight-hour day does not protect free time if the worker must finish the remaining work at home.

## Modernity and modernization

The lecture distinguishes **modernization** from **modernity**.

| Modernization | Modernity |
|---|---|
| Adoption of new goods, machines, infrastructure, and lifestyles | A social condition tied to freedom, mobility, dignity, and challenges to inherited authority |
| Often measured through technology or consumption | Judged through the quality and freedom of human life |
| Example: replacing walking with a motorbike | Example: workers gaining control over time and protection from degrading conditions |

Technological sophistication does not guarantee dignity. A smartphone can be technically advanced while the people assembling it face longer hours and harsher discipline than earlier industrial workers.

The Tricontinental study discussed in class argues that workers in Apple's production chain experience a rate of exploitation many times greater than that of nineteenth-century English factory workers. The exact calculation depends on the study's method. Its broader point is clear: technical progress can coexist with intensified exploitation.

## Global commodity chains

A **global commodity chain** divides the production of one commodity among many firms, workplaces, and countries.

An iPhone does not move from raw material to finished device on a single shop floor. Mining, component manufacture, design, assembly, software, logistics, marketing, and retail occur at different nodes.

Three systems make this geographical division possible:

1. Information and communication technology coordinates work across distance.
2. Finance moves rapidly between firms and countries.
3. Modern logistics moves materials, components, and finished goods between production nodes.

```text
Raw materials -> components -> assembly -> software -> logistics -> retail
       Each stage may occur in a different country or firm
```

This is both a technical and political arrangement. Firms can search globally for lower wages, weaker regulation, tax advantages, and workers with particular skills.

## Wages and socially necessary labor time

A **wage** is payment for labor power, commonly calculated by the day or month. It does not necessarily equal the total value created by the worker.

Marx uses **socially necessary labor time** to refer to the average labor time needed to produce a commodity under prevailing conditions of skill and technology. Competition pressures firms and workers to meet or beat that social average.

A wage can therefore hide two relationships:

- The worker appears to receive payment for all working hours.
- The owner retains the value created beyond what returns to the worker as wages.

## Unequal freedom within corporations

Modern firms do not treat all employees alike. Most workers follow assigned tasks, deadlines, and procedures. A smaller group receives room to experiment because the firm considers its members difficult to replace.

### Intrapreneurs

An **intrapreneur** is an employee permitted to act entrepreneurially within a corporation. Such workers may design new products, business models, or methods of extracting revenue.

They may receive:

- High salaries
- Stock or stock options
- Flexible working conditions
- Decision-making authority
- Public visibility and prestige

Companies offer these rewards to retain scarce knowledge and prevent employees from leaving to form competitors.

This is not equality within the workplace. It is a hierarchy based on replaceability and expected contribution to future profit.

### Designers of exploitation

Senior executives and highly valued technical workers may design systems through which the company captures value from other people's work, attention, or behavior. Their importance to investors lies less in technical brilliance by itself than in their ability to organize profitable extraction.

Even these workers do not receive the full value made possible by their creativity. Unless they own enough capital to control profits, they remain employees. Their alienation comes with far more privilege than that of an easily replaced worker.

## Data rent and platform labor

Social media feels free because users do not pay a direct monetary price for ordinary access. The lecture describes users' attention and activity as a form of **data rent**.

Users contribute:

- Time and attention
- Personal information
- Social connections
- Posts, images, and videos
- Behavioral data useful for advertising and prediction

Platforms convert these contributions into revenue. The user's activity is therefore economically useful even without a conventional wage relation.

The phrase "if you are not paying, you are the product" is too simple. Users are also workers, audiences, data sources, communities, and customers in different moments. The concept of data rent draws attention to the value captured through use.

## Prestige, merit, and the corporate ladder

Why might a student aspire to become a chief executive such as Sundar Pichai or Indra Nooyi? Money matters, but the role also carries:

- Social prestige
- Respectability
- Authority
- Valuable networks
- Recognition as someone who has "made it"

Corporate brands become status symbols. A company logo on a pen or T-shirt can carry prestige because people attach merit and success to membership in that institution.

The aspiration is not always to become a capitalist owner. Many students imagine becoming highly promoted workers with money, autonomy, and status.

## Capitalism's promise of exceptional mobility

Capitalism appears progressive when compared with feudalism because people are formally free to change jobs, move between places, own property, and attempt upward mobility. A serf tied to land did not possess these choices.

Formal freedom does not guarantee a good option. A worker may be free to refuse a job but lack food, housing, or another employer. Marx's critique asks what freedom means when survival depends on selling labor power.

### The exceptional success story

Capitalism advertises itself through exceptions:

- The worker who became a CEO
- The student who became an IAS officer
- The founder who entered Y Combinator
- The entrepreneur who built a major company from little capital

One person may genuinely succeed. The story becomes ideological when that exception is used to imply that everyone had an equal chance and that failure proves insufficient effort.

The sociological question is not only, "How did this person succeed?" It is also, "How many people performed similar labor but did not receive the same result, and why?"

## Illusions that stabilize capitalism

The class identified several beliefs that make exploitation easier to tolerate.

| Illusion | Promise | Hidden problem |
|---|---|---|
| **Choice** | A worker can always find another employer | Jobs may be scarce, insecure, or similarly exploitative |
| **Reward for effort** | Hard work will produce success | Rewards also depend on caste, class, networks, timing, and luck |
| **Individual freedom** | Anyone can climb the ladder alone | Structural inequality shapes access to every rung |
| **Superiority** | At least someone is below me | Relative privilege distracts from exploitation shared in unequal degrees |
| **Consumer freedom** | More products mean more freedom | Choice among commodities does not equal control over production or life |

These are not complete fabrications. People do change jobs, gain promotions, and enjoy consumer goods. The illusion lies in treating limited possibilities as universal and ignoring the structure that distributes them.

## Alienation and coping

Workers who recognize exploitation may be encouraged to cope with it individually through therapy, productivity systems, a new job, or a new institution.

These responses may help a person survive. They do not necessarily change the conditions producing distress. Moving from one prestigious institution to another may reproduce the same workload, competition, and isolation.

This is the difference between:

- Treating suffering as an individual's failure to cope
- Studying suffering as a symptom of how work is organized

Sociological skepticism needs some hope. If the system is treated as permanent and invulnerable, critique becomes another way to surrender to it.

## Consumption and commodity fetishism

**Commodity fetishism** occurs when relations among people appear as relations among things. Consumers encounter a finished commodity and forget the labor, extraction, and power relations that produced it.

An iPhone appears as a desirable object rather than a chain of miners, factory workers, engineers, logistics workers, marketers, and platform users.

### Retail therapy

**Retail therapy** uses shopping or consumption to manage sadness, stress, or frustration. Capitalism offers commodities as a response to distress that capitalist work may itself have helped produce.

```text
Work and competition produce distress
                 ↓
Consumption promises relief
                 ↓
More income and work are needed to consume
```

The relief can be real and still leave its cause untouched.

### The consumer's illusion of choice

A supermarket may offer an enormous number of cereal varieties. This looks like freedom, but all options remain within the role of consumer. The shopper chooses among products without deciding how work, wages, ownership, or distribution should be organized.

Capitalism encourages variety because firms compete for sales. It also tends toward overproduction. Unsold goods create losses, weaker firms disappear, and surviving firms gain greater market power.

## Monopoly capitalism

**Monopoly capitalism** develops when competition eliminates or absorbs many firms, leaving a small number with enough power to control a market.

Indian mobile telecommunications, dominated by a few providers, and periods of severe concentration in commercial aviation illustrate the problem. The system celebrates consumer choice while its competitive process can steadily reduce that choice.

```text
Competition -> overproduction and losses -> firm exits or mergers -> concentration
```

The result is a contradiction. Competition is presented as the source of choice, yet competition can produce monopoly or oligopoly.

## The appropriation of resistance

The lecture ended with a protest song about **jal, jangal, zameen**, meaning water, forest, and land. A singer asks a police officer to investigate the theft of a forest and river. The complaint is deliberately absurd at the literal level. Mining cannot carry a whole river away like a household object, yet it can destroy access to land, water, and forest life.

A recording of the song later circulates on Instagram and other platforms. This gives the protest a larger audience, but it also generates data and attention for corporations.

The example contains several relations at once:

- An Adivasi movement resists corporate mining.
- A journalist or artist records and circulates the resistance.
- A small creator may gain visibility or income.
- A platform captures attention and advertising value.
- The song's political message reaches people who would not otherwise hear it.

Even resistance can be appropriated by the system it criticizes. That does not make circulation useless or the creator immoral. It shows why exploitation, aspiration, and hope cannot be separated neatly.

Food delivered to a protest creates a similar contradiction. Delivery workers may face exploitation while enabling solidarity with protesters.

> [!note]
> Marxian analysis is strongest here as a method for tracing labor, value, and appropriation. It does not provide a simple formula for resolving every contradiction.

## Exam-ready synthesis

1. Labor power is the human capacity to work, and capital purchases its use for a period.
2. Surplus arises from labor performed beyond the value returned to the worker as wages.
3. Self-exploitation occurs when people internalize demands for productivity and extend work into personal time.
4. Modernization concerns technical and consumer change, while modernity also raises questions of dignity and freedom.
5. A global commodity chain divides production across firms and countries through ICT, finance, and logistics.
6. Corporations grant selected intrapreneurs autonomy and shares because their knowledge is difficult to replace.
7. Data rent describes value captured from users' attention, activity, and information.
8. Corporate prestige turns employment into a marker of merit and social success.
9. Capitalism promises mobility through exceptional success stories while hiding the many people who do not move upward.
10. Choice, effort, individual freedom, superiority, and consumption can operate as partial truths that conceal structural constraints.
11. Commodity fetishism hides the social relations and labor embodied in commodities.
12. Overproduction and competition can reduce consumer choice by producing monopoly or oligopoly.
13. Platforms can appropriate protest while also helping resistance circulate.

## Key terms

- **Labor power:** Human capacity to work, including skill, attention, energy, and creativity.
- **Necessary labor time:** Portion of work that produces value equivalent to the worker's wage.
- **Surplus labor time:** Portion of work that produces value retained by the owner.
- **Self-exploitation:** Internalized pressure through which people intensify or extend their own work.
- **Modernization:** Adoption of new technologies, infrastructure, commodities, and lifestyles.
- **Modernity:** Social condition associated with mobility, freedom, dignity, rationalization, and weakened inherited authority.
- **Global commodity chain:** Production network that divides work on one commodity across firms and countries.
- **Socially necessary labor time:** Average labor time required to produce a commodity under prevailing conditions.
- **Intrapreneur:** Employee given scope to develop entrepreneurial projects inside a firm.
- **Replaceability:** Degree to which an employer can substitute one worker for another.
- **Data rent:** Value a platform captures from users' attention, behavior, and information.
- **Meritocracy:** System that presents rewards as the result of individual ability and effort.
- **Alienation:** Separation from control over one's work, product, fellow workers, or human capacities.
- **Commodity fetishism:** Treatment of commodities as if their value were independent of the human relations that produced them.
- **Retail therapy:** Consumption used to manage emotional distress.
- **Monopoly capitalism:** Concentrated form of capitalism in which a small number of firms dominate markets.
- **Appropriation:** Capture and reuse of labor, culture, or resistance for another actor's benefit.
