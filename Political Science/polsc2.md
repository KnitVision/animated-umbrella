# The State: Theories and Critiques

> [!summary]
> The **idealist approach** explains *why the state is necessary*, while **social-contract theory** explains *how the state comes into being*. Both present the state as broadly benevolent. Feminist and Marxist approaches challenge this assumption by asking whose interests and ideas the state actually serves.

## Studying the State

The **state** is the formal political institution referred to when we say that countries such as India, Pakistan, or the United States act. It must not be confused with:

- A **nation**, which is a socially and institutionally constructed community.
- A **country**, the term commonly used for a political-territorial unit.
- A constituent unit such as the state of Rajasthan.

The state is central to political science because it is pervasive and difficult to escape. It regulates activities such as:

- Education and examinations
- Travel, passports, and driving
- Marriage and family relations
- Property ownership and building construction
- Public conduct and private activity
- Media and film censorship

Although political organization is ancient, the **modern state** is generally associated with developments after the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. It differs from earlier kingdoms, empires, and regimes in its institutions, reach, and organization.

### Normative and Empirical Approaches

| Approach | Central concern | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Normative** | Values, principles, and what political arrangements *ought* to be | The claim that democracy is desirable or that a pious king should rule |
| **Empirical** | Observable institutions, forms, processes, and actual outcomes | Asking what voting system a democracy uses or studying how kings have historically behaved |

Political analysis requires both. A normative commitment to democracy does not identify its institutional form; empirical analysis asks whether it uses direct voting, transferable votes, representatives, councils, or another arrangement.

## Hegel's Idealist View

Hegel explains the state through three stages, or **moments of social existence**, in which individuals progressively understand themselves in relation to larger institutions.

### 1. Family: Particular Altruism

The family is the individual's first social setting. A child initially experiences other people through care directed particularly toward them.

- Family members place the child's needs before their own.
- The child first recognizes the existence of others through this care.
- This is **particular altruism** because concern is directed toward specific members of a limited unit.

Hegel is concerned with the family's role in forming the individual, not primarily with whether it is nuclear, joint, or geographically dispersed.

### 2. Civil Society: Universal Egoism

As individuals move beyond the family, they encounter groups, associations, markets, and institutions that do not revolve around them.

- Each person must negotiate with others as an individual.
- People pursue their own needs within a wider social order.
- The individual recognizes that they are one member of a universal community.

This is **universal egoism**: the self recognizes its individuality and pursues its interests, but does so within society. Here, *egoism* means recognition of the self's position in relation to the universal, not ego in the everyday psychological sense.

### 3. State: Universal Altruism

The individual eventually recognizes an authority beyond both family and civil society. Adult activities such as voting, obtaining a licence or passport, signing official documents, and obeying law make this relationship visible.

- The state applies to everyone within its jurisdiction.
- It regulates family relations, civil society, and other institutions.
- Citizens accept responsibilities toward the state.
- The state is expected to protect and care for its citizens.

This is **universal altruism** because the state is imagined as caring for everyone under it, rather than for only a particular family or association.

| Moment | Institution | Principle | Individual's realization |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | Family | Particular altruism | Others care specifically for me |
| Second | Civil society | Universal egoism | I am an individual among other individuals and institutions |
| Third | State | Universal altruism | A universal authority governs and protects everyone, including me |

> [!important] Why this is idealist
> Hegel offers a normative account of the state's purpose rather than an empirical description of every actual state. He assumes that the state enables individuals to fully develop and acts for the universal good. This benevolent assumption is open to criticism.

## Social-Contract Tradition

Where Hegel asks **why the state is necessary**, social-contract theorists ask **how the state originates**. Thomas Hobbes, John Locke, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau each use the same three-part model:

1. **State of nature:** life without a state or government
2. **Reasoning:** the problem individuals recognize in that condition
3. **Contract:** the political authority they agree to create

The state of nature is a theoretical model. It imagines individuals, families, and perhaps simple social groups existing without a government or formal state.

## Hobbes

### State of Nature

Hobbes has the most pessimistic account of human beings. Individuals prioritize self-preservation and the survival of their families. Under scarce resources and without a common authority, everyone poses a potential threat to everyone else.

Life is therefore **solitary, nasty, brutish, and short**. Cooperation may occur, but it remains unreliable when interests conflict.

### Reasoning

Individuals realize that constant insecurity is intolerable. They cannot live well while always fearing theft, violence, or death. Their primary need is protection from one another.

### Contract: The Leviathan

Individuals surrender their unrestricted liberty to a powerful sovereign, the **Leviathan**, which:

- Creates universal laws and rules
- Determines right and wrong
- Punishes violations
- Protects each person from others
- Regulates liberty to make secure freedom possible

The apparent paradox is that people give up liberty in order to become free from fear. Hobbes does not trust individuals to protect either their own or others' well-being without coercive authority.

> [!warning] Criticism
> If people surrender all rights, the Leviathan may decide which rights to return and may remove liberties whenever it chooses. This differs from a liberal constitutional state, where some rights, such as the right to life, are protected from arbitrary state interference.

## Locke

### State of Nature

Locke's state of nature is generally peaceful. Human beings are productive and social: they work, form families, settle, build homes, and acquire property and wealth. Conflict is possible, but it is exceptional rather than constant.

### Reasoning

Once individuals have created a stable life, they need to **preserve and protect** it. The motivation for political authority is especially tied to protecting:

- **Life**
- **Liberty**
- **Property**

Unlike Hobbes, Locke does not begin with a universally violent world. He begins with a broadly good life that remains vulnerable to occasional interference.

### Contract: Minimal or Night-Watchman State

Individuals create a limited state whose main task is protection. It should enforce laws and preserve life, liberty, property, family, and wealth, but otherwise interfere as little as possible.

- The state is a **means** to protection, not an end in itself.
- Its authority is limited to the purpose for which it was created.
- If it violates rather than protects fundamental rights, individuals may resist it.

The lecture connects Locke's emphasis on property to colonialism: claims over land could be presented as legitimate property requiring state protection, even against Indigenous inhabitants or dispossessed groups. *Robinson Crusoe* was used as an illustration of this possessive, territorial logic.

## Rousseau

### State of Nature

Rousseau describes human beings as **noble savages**. They are capable of violence but also possess reason and a sense of good and bad. They form families and small institutions and can cooperate.

Over time, however, property and social development produce comparison:

- Some acquire more wealth or better property than others.
- Envy and jealousy emerge.
- Social inequality generates conflict.
- People who were born free become constrained by social structures: **"Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains."**

### Reasoning

Because people have already demonstrated the ability to associate in families and smaller institutions, they can create a larger political association. A state becomes necessary to manage conflict, protect the community, and address inequalities.

### Contract: Representative State and General Will

Rousseau rejects both Hobbes's unlimited sovereign and Locke's narrowly protective state. The state must continuously represent the **general will** of its people.

- Individuals must be represented in the state as they are in civil society.
- Public institutions should reflect collective needs and choices.
- Citizens communicate the general will through participation, elections, writing, pamphlets, organization, and other political activity.
- The state must keep listening; creating it is not a one-time transfer of decision-making power.

If the state begins serving only itself and no longer represents the general will, the old contract has failed. It must be reformed or replaced through a new contract. Where reform is impossible, this logic permits **revolution**.

> [!note] Power and representation
> Rousseau does not intend representatives to become an autonomous ruling group. Concentrated authority remains legitimate only while it continuously reflects the general will. A state that stops listening loses the basis of its legitimacy.

## Comparing the Contractualists

| Dimension | Hobbes | Locke | Rousseau |
|---|---|---|---|
| **View of humanity** | Primarily concerned with self-preservation; unreliable under conflict | Productive, social, and generally peaceful | Reasonable and social "noble savages," but vulnerable to envy |
| **State of nature** | Violent and insecure | Peaceful but vulnerable | Initially free and cooperative, then damaged by inequality |
| **Main problem** | Threats to life and survival from other people | Insecurity of life, liberty, property, and wealth | Inequality, envy, conflict, and failure of collective coordination |
| **Reason for the state** | Escape a terrible life | Preserve an already good life | Express collective will and manage social conflict |
| **Contract creates** | Leviathan or powerful sovereign | Minimal/night-watchman state | Representative state guided by the general will |
| **Extent of state power** | Extensive; the sovereign makes and enforces the rules | Limited to protecting fundamental interests | Broader than Locke's state but accountable to continuing participation |
| **Citizen's recourse** | Little or no resistance to the sovereign | Resistance when the state violates its protective purpose | Reform or replace the state when it ceases to represent the general will |

### Core Distinctions

- **Hobbes versus Locke:** Hobbes needs protection because life without authority is terrible; Locke needs protection to preserve a generally peaceful and productive life.
- **Locke versus Rousseau:** Locke focuses on preserving life, liberty, and property; Rousseau requires the state to respond to the evolving general will.
- **Hobbes versus Rousseau:** Hobbes transfers decision-making to the sovereign; Rousseau requires continuing democratic participation and representation.

These models simplify human nature by describing people as inherently selfish, productive, or noble. Their value lies in identifying recurring functions of modern states: protection, preservation of rights and property, lawmaking, and representation.

## Critiques of the Benevolent State

Hegel and the social-contract theorists generally imagine the state as caring for its subjects:

- Hobbes's state protects life.
- Locke's state protects rights and property.
- Rousseau's state represents collective interests.
- Hegel's state embodies universal care.

Critical approaches reject the assumption that the state neutrally serves everyone. They ask whether state institutions instead preserve particular hierarchies, classes, genders, or ideologies.

### Feminist or Patriarchal Critique

The feminist critique argues that states have been shaped by patriarchal institutions and masculine assumptions. Consequently, their laws and priorities may preserve gender inequality.

Examples discussed in the lecture include:

- Hostility toward women's rights or reluctance to advance gender equality
- Treating territorial control, military strength, and aggression as the main forms of security
- Spending far more on military institutions than on diplomacy
- Undervaluing communication, negotiation, and cooperation between states

The critique is not simply a demand for institutions labelled "feminine." It questions why aggression, borders, property, and military force are treated as natural foundations of politics, and proposes diplomacy and communication as alternative priorities.

### Marxist or Bourgeois Critique

The Marxist critique sees the state as representing the **ruling or propertied class** rather than society as a whole.

- State institutions primarily protect the interests of those who control wealth and property.
- Welfare schemes, legal rights, and protections may exist without changing the underlying class structure.
- The long-term effect of the state may be to preserve class inequality and the ruling class's control.

This interpretation challenges the idea of universal altruism: formally universal institutions can still produce unequal material benefits.

## Althusser: How the State Maintains Order

Louis Althusser distinguishes two kinds of **state apparatus**. Together, they explain how a social and economic order is maintained through both consent and coercion.

### Ideological State Apparatuses (ISA)

ISAs shape beliefs, values, identities, and ideas about proper behaviour. Examples include:

- Schools
- Religious organizations
- Other institutions that teach citizenship and nationalism

Practices such as uniforms, assemblies, pledges, patriotic songs, and lessons about service to the country train individuals to see particular conduct and political arrangements as normal. In a Marxist analysis, ideology can similarly present capitalism and the existing economic order as natural or beneficial.

### Repressive State Apparatuses (RSA)

RSAs secure obedience through force or the threat of force. Examples include:

- Police
- Military
- Other coercive agencies of the state

When ideological formation fails to secure conformity, repression may be used against dissent or protest.

| Apparatus | Main method | Examples | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ISA** | Ideology, socialization, and consent | Schools, religion, civic rituals | Produces ideas of the "good" citizen and acceptable social order |
| **RSA** | Coercion and repression | Police, military | Enforces order when consent or ideology fails |

> [!tip] Relationship between ISA and RSA
> The state first encourages people to internalize its preferred values and identities. When persuasion and socialization are insufficient, it can enforce compliance through coercion.

## Exam-Ready Synthesis

1. The state is a pervasive modern institution that regulates personal, social, economic, and political life.
2. Hegel explains **why** it is necessary: it is the highest stage of social existence and embodies universal altruism.
3. Social-contract theorists explain **how** it arises by moving from a state of nature, through reasoning, to a political contract.
4. Hobbes creates a powerful Leviathan for security; Locke creates a minimal state to protect life, liberty, and property; Rousseau creates a representative state accountable to the general will.
5. Feminist and Marxist critiques reject the image of a neutral, universally benevolent state.
6. Althusser explains state power through ideological apparatuses that produce consent and repressive apparatuses that enforce compliance.

## Key Terms

- **State:** Formal political institution exercising authority over a population and territory.
- **Normative:** Concerned with values and what ought to be.
- **Empirical:** Concerned with observable institutions, processes, and outcomes.
- **Particular altruism:** Care directed toward members of the family.
- **Universal egoism:** Individual self-interest pursued within civil society.
- **Universal altruism:** The state's idealized care for all under its authority.
- **State of nature:** Hypothetical condition without government or a state.
- **Leviathan:** Hobbes's powerful sovereign created through the surrender of liberty.
- **Night-watchman state:** Locke's limited state focused on protection.
- **General will:** Rousseau's conception of the collective will that must guide the state.
- **ISA:** Institutions that reproduce political and social order primarily through ideology.
- **RSA:** Institutions that enforce political and social order primarily through coercion.
