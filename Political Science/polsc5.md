# Citizenship: Membership, Entitlements, and Models

> [!summary]
> **Citizenship** is membership in a political community, ordinarily the state. It creates a direct relationship between the individual and the state and carries rights, duties, protections, and expectations. Citizenship must be studied through two questions: **Who is recognized as a citizen?** and **What does being a citizen entail?** Legal status alone does not guarantee substantive equality: people may be formal citizens yet remain unable to enjoy citizenship fully because of gender, class, caste, religion, ethnicity, disability, or migration.

## The Two Questions of Citizenship

| Question | Focus | Typical issues |
|---|---|---|
| **Who is a citizen?** | Rules of membership | Birth, descent, registration, naturalization, migration, documentation, and statelessness |
| **What does citizenship entail?** | Content of membership | Rights, liberties, participation, duties, welfare, recognition, and belonging |

The questions are connected but analytically distinct. The first determines entry into the category of citizen; the second asks what status, practices, and relationships follow from entry.

## A Working Definition

Citizenship has three classical elements:

1. **Membership:** recognition as a member of a political community.
2. **A direct citizen-state relationship:** citizens make claims on the state, while the state exercises authority over and assumes responsibilities toward them.
3. **Entitlements:** the rights, protections, benefits, duties, and legitimate expectations attached to membership.

Here, **entitlement** is broader than a benefit. It may involve something the state owes the citizen, something the citizen may claim, or an obligation the citizen is expected to perform.

Citizens also have **civil relationships** with one another, but citizenship specifically highlights their political and legal relationship with the state described in [[polsc3|State and Government]].

> [!important] Citizenship and nationality
> The words are often used interchangeably, especially in passport law, but they can be distinguished analytically. **Citizenship** emphasizes political and legal membership, rights, and participation; **nationality** can also refer to legal allegiance or cultural-national identity.

## Who Is a Citizen?

### 1. Citizenship by Birth: *Jus Soli*

**Jus soli** means the **right of the soil**: citizenship follows from birth within a state's territory.

- It treats territorial birth as the basis of membership.
- It can include a child even when the parents are not citizens.
- The United States is a prominent example of a broad birthright principle.
- Indian citizenship at the commencement of the Republic was substantially more birth- and domicile-oriented than it later became.

The principle raises difficult cases:

- Should a child born during a parent's brief visit receive citizenship?
- What happens when birth occurs in a disputed territory?
- Is place of birth a sufficient indication of social belonging?
- How is birth proved when a person lacks a birth certificate?

### 2. Citizenship by Descent: *Jus Sanguinis*

**Jus sanguinis** means the **right of blood**: citizenship follows from one or both parents.

- It connects membership to lineage rather than territory.
- It can transmit citizenship to children born abroad.
- It may preserve membership across a diaspora.
- Historically, descent rules have often been patriarchal, transmitting status through fathers rather than mothers.

Anupama Roy shows that Indian law gradually shifted toward descent:

| Period of birth in India | General rule under the Citizenship Act |
|---|---|
| **26 January 1950 to 30 June 1987** | Birth in India generally conferred citizenship, subject to limited exceptions. |
| **1 July 1987 to 2 December 2004** | At least one parent had to be an Indian citizen. |
| **From 3 December 2004** | Both parents must be citizens, or one must be a citizen and the other must not be an illegal migrant. |

This movement from broad *jus soli* toward *jus sanguinis* illustrates a **closure** of citizenship by birth: ancestry and the legal status of parents increasingly determine a child's membership.

### 3. Citizenship by Registration

**Registration** is a formal statutory route through which a person belonging to an eligible category applies to be entered as a citizen.

- Eligibility may depend on residence, Indian origin, marriage, age, or another legally specified connection.
- The emphasis is on satisfying documentary and procedural conditions.
- It does not necessarily require the cultural tests associated with naturalization.

Registration is not simply automatic residence-based citizenship. A person must belong to a category created by law and complete the prescribed process.

### 4. Citizenship by Naturalization

**Naturalization** is the acquisition of citizenship after demonstrating a sufficiently durable connection to the country.

Requirements vary by state and may include:

- A minimum period of lawful residence
- Knowledge of an official or national language
- Familiarity with political institutions or civic life
- Good character or a clean criminal record
- An oath of allegiance
- An intention to remain in the country
- Renunciation of an earlier citizenship where dual citizenship is restricted

The concept assumes that the applicant has become integrated into the political community. Germany's language requirements illustrate how cultural integration may be made a condition of legal membership. The exact period and tests are procedural choices, not universal features of naturalization.

### 5. Citizenship by Incorporation of Territory

When territory becomes part of a state, the law may recognize its residents as citizens through an **incorporation order**. In India, citizenship orders were used in relation to territories including Goa, Daman and Diu, Dadra and Nagar Haveli, Puducherry, and Sikkim.

This is an exceptional route associated with territorial and constitutional change rather than an ordinary individual application.

### The Routes Can Overlap

These pathways are ideal types, not mutually exclusive boxes. A state may require a combination of:

- Birth in the territory and residence for a specified period
- Descent and formal registration
- Residence, language proficiency, and an oath
- Marriage and a minimum period of residence

The substantive basis and the procedure must be separated. **Birth** is a substantive ground; presenting a valid birth certificate is the procedure used to establish it. **Descent** is a substantive ground; proving the parents' citizenship is procedural.

## Substantive and Procedural Citizenship

| Dimension | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Substantive principle** | The larger normative reason for treating someone as a member | Birth, descent, residence, integration, or political association |
| **Procedure** | The legal method used to verify and confer membership | Certificates, registers, residence periods, tests, applications, and tribunals |

Procedures are shaped by history, politics, administrative capacity, and local conflict. The same substantive principle can therefore produce different rules in different states.

There is also a second and deeper use of **substantive citizenship**: the actual ability to enjoy the rights, respect, security, and social standing promised by legal membership. A person may satisfy every formal procedure and still lack substantive equality.

> [!example] Formal status versus lived citizenship
> A person may legally possess citizenship but be prevented by discrimination, poverty, inaccessible institutions, or unequal laws from exercising political rights, owning property, moving freely, or receiving state protection. Such a person is sometimes described as a **second-class** or **incomplete citizen**.

## Problems of Classification

### Statelessness

**Statelessness** is the condition of not being recognized as a national by any state under its law. A stateless person may lack:

- A regular passport
- Secure residence
- Freedom of international movement
- Political rights
- Reliable access to education, employment, or welfare
- Diplomatic protection
- An officially recognized political home

The lecture's example of a Tibetan-origin woman born in India shows how gaps between territory, descent, refugee status, and recognition can leave a person without effective citizenship. Even ordinary acts such as selecting a country in an online form or travelling to a conference become difficult. Her eventual naturalization in France was not merely an exchange of benefits; it supplied legal identity, mobility, recognition, and security.

### Refugees Are Not Automatically Citizens

A **refugee** is a person who has crossed a border because of persecution, conflict, or serious danger and seeks protection outside their country. Refugee status can provide protection or residence without full political membership.

- Long residence does not automatically produce citizenship.
- A host state may provide documents without granting a passport.
- Acquiring the host state's citizenship may be politically painful if it appears to weaken a claim to return or to an occupied homeland.
- A refugee can therefore remain in a prolonged condition of uncertain or deferred membership.

### Disputed Territories and Unrecognized States

Territory-based and descent-based rules assume that birthplace, parentage, and nationality can be clearly identified. Disputed or unrecognized territories expose the weakness of this assumption. A state may control a territory without broad international recognition, while residents may possess documents that other states do not treat as ordinary passports.

### The Documentation Trap

Citizenship may appear passive until it is questioned. Once challenged, a person must produce documents generated by the very state whose recognition is in dispute. Missing records, inconsistent borders, displacement, poverty, and historical discrimination can therefore convert an abstract status into a **Catch-22**.

The lecture compares this to losing one's phone and wallet: the importance of recognized identity becomes visible when a person suddenly cannot prove who they are or make ordinary claims on others.

## Formal Citizenship and Recognition

From the late twentieth century, feminist and multicultural scholarship emphasized that citizenship is not exhausted by membership, state relations, and legal entitlements. Citizenship also provides:

- **Meaning:** a recognized position within political and social life
- **Respect:** treatment as a person whose claims matter
- **Recognition:** public acknowledgement of identity and equal standing
- **Belonging:** a secure political home and participation in a shared community

The absence, threatened withdrawal, or unequal enjoyment of citizenship reveals these functions most clearly. Without recognition, a person may be treated as a non-entity even while residing for decades within a state.

### T.H. Marshall: Equal Membership

**T.H. Marshall** defined citizenship as a status bestowed on full members of a community, whose holders are equal with respect to the rights and duties attached to that status. He divided citizenship into three elements:

| Element | Content | Associated institutions |
|---|---|---|
| **Civil citizenship** | Rights necessary for individual freedom: liberty, speech, conscience, property, contract, and access to justice | Courts and the legal system |
| **Political citizenship** | Participation in political power as voter, representative, organizer, or public actor | Parliament, elections, and representative institutions |
| **Social citizenship** | Economic security, welfare, education, and the ability to share in society's social heritage | Welfare and educational institutions |

Marshall's framework explains why the right to vote alone does not produce full citizenship. Civil freedom may be ineffective without political voice, while both may be hollow without the social conditions needed to exercise them.

> [!note] Limits of Marshall
> Marshall describes an egalitarian expansion of citizenship, but formal equality can coexist with capitalism and entrenched inequalities of class, caste, race, religion, and gender. Social rights may soften inequality without dismantling its underlying structure.

## Anupama Roy: Citizenship as Promise and Hierarchy

Roy presents citizenship as simultaneously inclusive and exclusive.

### Citizenship as a Momentum Concept

A **momentum concept** contains an internal pressure toward expansion. Struggles by excluded people can extend citizenship to more persons and deepen the rights attached to it. Citizenship therefore has no final, complete form: each achievement creates a basis for further claims to equality.

### Encompassment and Closure

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Encompassment** | The expansion of citizenship so that universal equality recognizes and includes differently situated people and groups |
| **Closure** | The denial, restriction, or unequal distribution of membership and its benefits through legal or social boundaries |

The two processes often occur together. A law can include one category while making another category more vulnerable. For example, the creation of Overseas Citizenship of India expanded ties to parts of the diaspora while citizenship by birth was becoming more restrictive and tied to descent.

### Constitutive Outsider and Forclusion

Citizenship defines not only who belongs but also who does not. The **constitutive outsider** is the alien, migrant, refugee, or allegedly deficient citizen against whom the secure citizen is identified.

Roy uses **forclusion** to describe more than simple exclusion. The outsider remains continually present in legal and political discourse as the contrast through which the citizen's authenticity is established, yet is prevented from becoming an equal citizen.

### Masked or Abstract Citizen

Liberal citizenship imagines an **abstract**, **unmarked**, or **masked citizen** whose caste, class, gender, race, religion, and culture are treated as irrelevant. This can be emancipatory because the law no longer openly assigns status by inherited hierarchy. It can also conceal inequality because people do not possess equal material ability to exercise formally identical rights.

## Differentiated Citizenship

**Differentiated citizenship** recognizes that uniform treatment may reproduce inequality. Equal citizenship may require special rights, representation, protection, or resources for groups placed at a historical disadvantage.

Indian constitutional examples include:

- **Articles 14-16:** equality and non-discrimination alongside provisions permitting affirmative measures for disadvantaged groups
- **Article 17:** abolition of untouchability
- **Articles 25-30:** religious and cultural rights, including minority protections
- **Directive Principles:** directions toward social welfare and the reduction of material inequality

This creates two subjects of rights:

1. The **individual citizen**, treated as an equal bearer of rights.
2. The **community**, recognized as a relevant unit whose language, culture, or disadvantaged position may require protection.

### The Dilemma of Group Rights

Group protection can make universal equality effective, but it can also empower dominant members within a community to impose **internal restrictions** on women, dissenters, minorities within minorities, or those who reject the group's dominant practices.

The central question is:

> How can the state recognize cultural difference without allowing community power to extinguish individual freedom and equality?

The North Sentinel Island discussion illustrates this dilemma. Non-interference can express respect for an Indigenous community's autonomy, yet complete non-interference cannot automatically become a universal principle where internal practices violate the rights of vulnerable members.

## What Does Citizenship Entail?

Citizenship can involve several kinds of claims and obligations:

- **Civil rights:** life, liberty, equality before law, speech, conscience, movement, and access to justice
- **Political rights:** voting, contesting elections, political organization, assembly, protest, campaigning, and public expression
- **Economic rights and opportunities:** work, livelihood, property under applicable law, and access to public employment where reserved for citizens
- **Social rights:** education, welfare, health, security, and the material basis for a dignified life
- **Protection:** domestic legal protection, passports, consular assistance, and diplomatic representation abroad
- **Duties:** obeying law, paying applicable taxes, respecting others' rights, and performing legally required civic obligations

Not all fundamental rights in India are exclusive to citizens. **Article 21's protection of life and personal liberty applies to every person**, while rights such as the freedoms in Article 19 are constitutionally framed for citizens.

## Three Models of Citizenship

These models emphasize different answers to what citizenship entails. Actual states normally combine them.

### 1. Liberal Citizenship

The **liberal model** treats citizenship principally as a legal status that secures individual liberty.

Core features:

- The individual is the primary unit.
- The state must protect rights and freedoms.
- Citizenship may be comparatively passive: the citizen receives protection without being required to participate constantly.
- Police, courts, and defence institutions protect life, liberty, and property.
- A welfare-state interpretation extends protection to social and economic security.

This model resembles **Locke's** limited state in [[polsc2|The State: Theories and Critiques]], created to preserve life, liberty, and property.

**Strength:** It protects the individual against both state power and social coercion.

**Limit:** Equal legal rights may remain merely formal when citizens possess radically unequal resources or social standing.

### 2. Republican Citizenship

The **republican model** treats citizenship as active participation in collective self-government.

Core features:

- Citizens are not merely recipients of state protection; they help constitute the state.
- Participation includes more than voting.
- Citizens deliberate, organize, protest, publish, campaign, hold office, and monitor representatives.
- Political freedom means sharing in the exercise and direction of public power.
- The state must remain responsive to the citizens' will.

This model resembles **Rousseau's** emphasis on the **general will**. Citizens remain political authors of the state and may reform or replace institutions that cease to represent them.

Switzerland's referendums and other mechanisms of direct participation are commonly used as a republican example.

**Strength:** It prevents citizenship from becoming a passive legal label.

**Limit:** Participation requires time, resources, information, and access that are distributed unequally. A supposed general will may also silence minorities.

### 3. Communitarian Citizenship

The **communitarian model** emphasizes membership in a community that has contributed to the individual's identity and well-being. Citizenship therefore carries duties to sustain the community.

Core features:

- Rights are accompanied by moral obligations.
- Citizens are expected to give back to the political community.
- Duties may include voting, paying taxes, public service, learning a language, or military service.
- The model values solidarity, shared identity, and common practices.

Examples include:

- **Compulsory military service**, where citizens must contribute directly to national defence
- Language requirements in **Quebec**, intended to preserve the province's French-speaking identity
- Requirements that public-facing workers learn a regional language, such as the lecture's example involving Marathi and taxi or auto-rickshaw drivers

**Strength:** It recognizes that political communities depend on cooperation and cannot be sustained entirely through individual claims.

**Limits:**

- Majority values can be imposed on minorities.
- Cultural conformity may be treated as proof of loyalty.
- Food, language, religion, dress, or family practices may become tests of the "good citizen."
- Group morality may undermine individual liberty and minority rights.

## Comparing the Three Models

| Dimension | Liberal | Republican | Communitarian |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Central value** | Liberty | Participation | Community and duty |
| **Citizen's role** | Rights-bearing individual | Active political participant | Responsible community member |
| **State's primary role** | Protect rights and liberties | Enable and respond to self-government | Sustain shared institutions and values |
| **Direction of relationship** | Primarily state protection of citizen | Citizens shape and supervise the state | Reciprocal obligations between citizen and community |
| **Typical danger** | Passive or merely formal citizenship | Exclusion from participation or domination by the majority | Moral conformity and suppression of minorities |
| **Associated theorist** | Locke | Rousseau | No single theorist; a family of community-centred approaches |

## India as a Mixed Model

India does not fit a single model:

- It is **liberal** because the Constitution protects fundamental rights and limits public power.
- It is **republican** because universal adult franchise, political freedoms, elections, assembly, protest, and public office enable participation.
- It contains **communitarian** features through constitutional duties, expectations of civic service, and protections for cultural and religious communities.
- It contains **social-citizenship** commitments through affirmative action and the Directive Principles' welfare goals.

The models are not always compatible. Individual liberty may conflict with community norms; majority rule may conflict with minority rights; a demand for duties may be used to weaken rights.

> [!warning] Constitutional correction
> The lecture transcript places citizens' duties in the Directive Principles. Formally, **Fundamental Duties are in Part IVA, Article 51A**. The **Directive Principles of State Policy are in Part IV, Articles 36-51**, and primarily direct the state toward social, economic, and political justice. Both express normative expectations, but they address different constitutional actors.

## Citizenship Is Not a Closed List of Citizen-Only Rights

Legal systems sometimes extend political or social entitlements to non-citizens. The lecture notes that eligible **Commonwealth citizens resident in the United Kingdom** may vote in certain elections and may be eligible for public office even without British citizenship. This demonstrates that citizenship, residence, nationality, and political rights do not always overlap perfectly.

Conversely, formal citizens may be denied effective political rights through discrimination, lack of documentation, displacement, or unequal access to institutions.

## Feminist Critique of Citizenship

Feminist theory asks whether supposedly universal membership rules were built around a male citizen.

- Property ownership historically favoured men.
- Descent was often traced through the father.
- The public citizen was imagined as economically independent, while unpaid care work was treated as private and politically irrelevant.
- Community rights can allow patriarchal authorities to control women in the name of cultural preservation.
- Equal rules may fail to address unequal social conditions.

This critique does not simply seek to add women to an unchanged category. It asks whether the definition of citizenship itself must incorporate **care, dependence, embodiment, and unequal power**.

A care-based conception argues that citizenship should include a collective responsibility to ensure that all members can live with dignity, rather than treating citizenship solely as an exchange of rights for duties.

## Multicultural Critique

Multicultural theory argues that a citizen's cultural, linguistic, religious, and ethnic attachments cannot always be erased in the name of abstract equality.

- Uniform law can advantage the majority culture while presenting itself as neutral.
- Recognition may require minority-language rights, cultural institutions, or group representation.
- However, cultural recognition must remain compatible with the freedom and equality of individuals within groups.

The goal is neither forced assimilation nor unquestioned community autonomy, but a **differentiated universalism** in which common citizenship takes social differences seriously.

## Citizenship as Passive Status and Active Practice

Citizenship is often experienced passively. A person receives it at birth and may rarely need to prove it. The status becomes active when the citizen:

- Votes or contests an election
- Protests or petitions government
- Makes a rights claim in court
- Pays taxes or performs public service
- Uses a passport or seeks consular assistance
- Organizes with others to change public institutions

The republican view emphasizes these practices, while critical theories add that marginalized people can reshape citizenship through struggles for recognition and rights. Citizenship is therefore both an **instituted legal status** and an **ongoing political process**.

## Central Tensions

### Universal Equality versus Social Difference

Should law ignore caste, gender, religion, and ethnicity to treat everyone alike, or recognize these differences to remedy historical inequality?

### Rights versus Duties

Are citizens primarily protected individuals, active political authors, or members who owe service to a community?

### Individual versus Community

When should individual liberty override cultural autonomy, and when does neutral individualism merely conceal majority privilege?

### Territory versus Descent

Should membership follow residence and birth in a territory, or ancestry and inherited belonging?

### Inclusion versus Closure

Every definition of membership identifies an outsider. The problem is whether boundaries are justified, proportionate, and compatible with human dignity.

### Legal Status versus Lived Equality

Does possession of citizenship documents make someone a full citizen when discrimination or poverty prevents the effective use of rights?

## Exam-Ready Synthesis

1. Citizenship asks both **who belongs** and **what belonging entails**.
2. Its classical definition combines membership, a direct citizen-state relationship, and entitlements.
3. Citizenship may be acquired through birth, descent, registration, naturalization, or incorporation of territory.
4. *Jus soli* bases citizenship on territory; *jus sanguinis* bases it on descent.
5. Substantive principles justify membership, while procedures provide the legal means to prove or acquire it.
6. Formal citizenship does not guarantee substantive citizenship, recognition, or equal enjoyment of rights.
7. Marshall divides citizenship into civil, political, and social elements.
8. The liberal model emphasizes liberty, the republican model participation, and the communitarian model duty and solidarity.
9. India combines all three models, along with constitutional recognition of both individual and community rights.
10. Differentiated citizenship seeks genuine equality by recognizing group disadvantage, but group rights can also produce internal domination.
11. Feminist theory exposes the patriarchal assumptions in property, descent, public participation, and the public-private divide.
12. Roy describes citizenship as a field of both **encompassment** and **closure**, with an egalitarian momentum that coexists with hierarchy and exclusion.
13. Stateless people and refugees reveal that citizenship supplies identity, mobility, protection, recognition, and a political home, not merely a package of benefits.
14. Citizenship remains an ongoing political struggle over the meaning and terms of equal membership.

## Key Terms

- **Citizenship:** Legal and political membership in a community, ordinarily a state, carrying rights, duties, protections, and expectations.
- **Membership:** Recognition that a person belongs to a political community.
- **Entitlement:** A right, protection, benefit, claim, expectation, or obligation arising from a relationship.
- **Jus soli:** Citizenship based on birth within a state's territory; the right of the soil.
- **Jus sanguinis:** Citizenship based on descent from citizen parents; the right of blood.
- **Registration:** Acquisition of citizenship by an eligible person through a prescribed statutory application.
- **Naturalization:** Acquisition of citizenship after satisfying residence, integration, and other legal requirements.
- **Incorporation of territory:** Conferment of citizenship following the addition of territory to a state.
- **Substantive principle:** The normative ground used to justify membership, such as birth, descent, residence, or association.
- **Procedure:** The legal and administrative means used to verify or confer citizenship.
- **Formal citizenship:** Legally recognized status as a citizen.
- **Substantive citizenship:** Effective enjoyment of rights, equality, dignity, recognition, and belonging.
- **Stateless person:** A person not considered a national by any state under the operation of its law.
- **Refugee:** A person outside their country who requires protection because of persecution, conflict, or serious danger.
- **Civil citizenship:** Rights necessary for individual freedom and legal personhood.
- **Political citizenship:** The right and capacity to participate in the exercise of political power.
- **Social citizenship:** Access to economic security, welfare, education, and the social conditions of equal membership.
- **Differentiated citizenship:** Group-sensitive rights or measures intended to make universal citizenship effective for disadvantaged people.
- **Second-class citizen:** A formal citizen who is denied equal status or effective access to citizenship's rights and benefits.
- **Liberal citizenship:** Citizenship centred on individual rights and state protection of liberty.
- **Republican citizenship:** Citizenship centred on active participation in collective self-government.
- **Communitarian citizenship:** Citizenship centred on community membership, solidarity, duties, and shared values.
- **Recognition:** Public and institutional acknowledgement of a person's identity and equal standing.
- **Momentum concept:** A concept whose egalitarian logic generates continuing struggles for expansion and deeper equality.
- **Encompassment:** Expansion of universal citizenship through recognition and inclusion of differently situated members.
- **Closure:** Restriction or denial of membership or its effective benefits.
- **Constitutive outsider:** The excluded or suspect figure against whom the identity of the citizen is defined.
- **Forclusion:** A relationship in which the outsider remains constitutively present in definitions of the citizen but is prevented from becoming equal.
- **Masked citizen:** The abstract liberal citizen whose social differences are treated as irrelevant, potentially concealing structural inequality.
