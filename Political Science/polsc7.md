# Power: Relations, Resistance, and Social Control

> [!summary]
> **Power** is the capacity to realize an objective despite resistance. It exists in relationships and can operate through law, force, wealth, knowledge, persuasion, surveillance, or social norms. Weber studies actors carrying out their will within social relationships. Foucault shows how institutions and norms lead people to regulate themselves even without a visible command.

> [!info] Sources
> The main discussion comes from Lecture 7 and the course note on power. **[Book: Roy]** marks applications from Anupama Roy's *Mapping Citizenship in India*. Historical clarification about Bentham's panopticon has been added.

## Politics as the study of power

Earlier lectures approached politics through the state, government, and citizenship. Another major approach treats politics as the study of **power and its manifestations**.

It asks:

- How does the state shape citizens' conduct?
- How do police, courts, militaries, and bureaucracies secure compliance?
- Why can some social groups influence decisions more easily than others?
- How do states influence one another?
- How do families, workplaces, markets, religions, and social norms regulate behaviour?
- How do people resist or reproduce power?

Terms such as **superpower**, **rising power**, and **government in power** show how common the concept is. Political analysis, however, requires more precision than everyday usage.

## Defining power

The lecture uses a broad definition:

> **Power is the capacity to realize an objective despite resistance or opposition.**

This definition contains:

1. An **actor**, such as an individual, group, institution, or state.
2. An **objective** the actor seeks.
3. **Resistance** that obstructs the objective.
4. A **capacity** to affect the outcome despite that resistance.

Power can operate through:

- Law and formal rules
- Violence or threats
- Rewards and incentives
- Persuasion and negotiation
- Wealth and control of resources
- Knowledge and expertise
- Surveillance
- Social expectations

These are means of power, not power itself. The important question is whether an actor can shape conduct or outcomes.

### Violence is not identical to power

Violence may punish, deter, silence, or produce obedience. Whether it succeeds depends on the objective. If repression strengthens resistance rather than securing compliance, force has been used but the intended power has not been fully realized.

The lecture's classroom example makes the same point. A teacher may ban devices to make students learn. Students may obey visibly while paying no attention. The rule changes conduct but fails to achieve the larger objective.

## Capacity, exercise, and outcome

Three ideas should be separated:

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Potential power** | Possession of resources or authority that could affect others | A traffic officer may issue penalties |
| **Exercise of power** | Use of those resources to shape conduct | The officer directs traffic or issues a fine |
| **Effective power** | Successful production of the intended result | Drivers obey rules and traffic becomes orderly |

A political party "comes to power" when it forms the government and gains legal authority. This does not mean it can achieve every policy goal. Holding office, possessing capacity, exercising power, and reaching an objective are related but distinct.

An actor may also choose not to exercise power. A credible threat can change behaviour without being carried out.

### Declared and hidden objectives

Power is difficult to evaluate when official and actual goals differ. Governments announce purposes through laws, policies, manifestos, and public statements, but critics may identify strategic or economic motives.

Analysis should compare declared aims with conduct, resource allocation, beneficiaries, costs, and outcomes. Claims about hidden motives require evidence. Failure to reach an announced goal might show weak power, poor policy design, conflicting objectives, or a concealed objective.

## Power is relational

Power does not exist in isolation. It operates through relationships:

- Individual and individual
- Individual and group
- Institution and individual
- Institution and group
- Institution and institution
- State and citizen
- State and state

An **institution** is a formally organized group with recognized positions and procedures, such as a university, court, ministry, or corporation. A social group may have less formal boundaries.

Power is relative to context. A professor has power in a classroom but remains subject to a dean, university regulations, and state law. A wealthy person may dominate an economic relationship but have no legal authority in court.

Hierarchies therefore matter without making lower actors powerless. People can evade rules, withhold cooperation, reinterpret instructions, exploit gaps, or organize collectively.

## Max Weber on power

**Max Weber** studied institutions, bureaucracy, rationalization, and social organization. He defines power as:

> [!quote]
> The probability that an actor within a social relationship can carry out their will despite resistance, regardless of the basis on which that probability rests.

The definition emphasizes:

1. Power exists within a **social relationship**.
2. Actors can seek incompatible outcomes.
3. Resistance is expected.
4. Power is the chance of carrying out one's will despite resistance.
5. Power may rest on many different resources.

### Sources of power

Power may be supported by:

- Wealth and material resources
- Knowledge and expertise
- Organizational position
- Social status and prestige
- Political office
- Religious standing
- Administrative control
- Coercive force

Resources matter only through relationships in which they can influence others.

### Resistance from below

Formal hierarchy does not eliminate resistance. Students may work around attendance rules, workers may slow production, citizens may evade regulations, and officials may reinterpret policy during implementation.

Power is therefore an interaction. Authorities anticipate resistance and change incentives, rules, or enforcement. Subordinate actors adapt in response.

### Power, domination, and authority

- **Power** is the capacity to carry out an objective despite resistance.
- **Domination** is a stable relationship in which commands are likely to be obeyed.
- **Authority** is a recognized or legitimate right to command.

An isolated success is an exercise of power. An institution that regularly secures obedience has a structure of domination. When people accept its right to command, domination becomes legitimate authority.

## Nonviolent resistance

Nonviolent action demonstrates why coercive resources do not guarantee effective power. Colonial or authoritarian authorities may use violence to produce fear and submission. Protesters can refuse the expected obedience.

Nonviolence may:

- Deny cooperation
- Make repression politically costly
- Expose the limits of coercion
- Attract support
- Change how the conflict is understood

The state retains weapons and formal authority, but its ability to realize its objective can decline.

## Michel Foucault on power

**Michel Foucault** moves beyond the idea that power belongs only to sovereign rulers or formal offices.

For Foucault:

- Power is dispersed through social relationships.
- Schools, hospitals, prisons, workplaces, and families organize conduct.
- Observation, classification, examination, and routines produce discipline.
- Power creates standards of normal and deviant behaviour.
- People internalize norms and monitor themselves.
- Individuals experience, reproduce, negotiate, and resist power.

Power does not only prohibit actions. It also produces identities, habits, categories, and ideas about normal conduct.

### Power-knowledge

Foucault treats **power and knowledge** as mutually productive. Institutions use knowledge to classify people, while those categories make intervention and supervision possible.

Examples include:

- Examinations ranking students
- Medical categories defining normal and pathological conditions
- Prison records classifying offenders by risk
- Population statistics dividing people by residence, work, caste, or religion
- Documents determining eligibility for rights or welfare

Such knowledge may be accurate and useful while still shaping who becomes visible, normal, eligible, risky, or governable.

## The panopticon

The **panopticon** was a prison design proposed by **Jeremy Bentham**. Foucault later used it as a model of disciplinary power in *Discipline and Punish*.

Cells surround a central watchtower. An observer can potentially see prisoners, but prisoners cannot know when anyone is watching.

1. Observation is always possible.
2. Prisoners cannot verify whether it is occurring.
3. They behave as if surveillance is continuous.
4. External monitoring becomes self-monitoring.

The defining mechanism is **asymmetrical visibility**, not constant physical force.

The same principle appears when students alter behaviour because an exam may be monitored, workers respond to performance tracking, or citizens censor themselves because employers, platforms, neighbours, or governments may observe them.

## Social norms and reproduction of power

People often regulate themselves without a direct command. Expectations about gender, marriage, clothing, sexuality, caste, careers, or public affection shape behaviour because individuals anticipate judgment or exclusion.

Power is reproduced through a cycle:

1. A norm defines proper conduct.
2. Institutions and communities reward conformity or punish deviation.
3. Individuals anticipate these responses.
4. They regulate themselves and others.
5. The norm persists without continuous direction from one authority.

Patriarchy, for example, is not maintained only through commands by men. People of different genders may enforce the same norms because those expectations appear natural or respectable.

## Roy: governmentality and citizenship

**[Book: Roy]** Roy applies this view of power through **governmentality**, the practices by which states count, classify, supervise, and administer populations.

Examples include:

- Citizenship registers and identity documents
- Census categories
- Rules separating citizens, residents, migrants, and foreigners
- Border and movement controls
- Welfare eligibility based on residence or documentation

These practices do not merely record a population. They create official categories and attach different rights, mobility, and recognition to them.

Following Partha Chatterjee, Roy calls enumerated population categories **bounded serialities**. They make administration possible but can also confine political identities and produce unequal citizenship.

Citizens resist through **people's practices of citizenship**. They challenge official classifications and force institutions to redefine rights. This supports the lecture's central point that power relationships include resistance.

## Weber and Foucault compared

| Dimension | Weber | Foucault |
|---|---|---|
| **Main focus** | An actor carrying out their will within a social relationship | Dispersed practices shaping conduct, knowledge, and norms |
| **Resistance** | Opposition an actor may overcome | Present throughout power relations |
| **Typical setting** | Institutions, bureaucracy, class, status, and politics | Prisons, schools, hospitals, surveillance, and everyday norms |
| **Mechanism** | Resources used to achieve an objective | Observation, classification, normalization, and self-discipline |
| **Individual** | Actor with objectives and resources | Both a subject and a transmitter of power |

Weber is useful when actors, resources, objectives, and resistance can be identified. Foucault is useful when surveillance, expertise, and norms operate across many institutions without a single commander.

## Manifestations of power

### Agenda-setting

Agenda-setting determines which problems and alternatives receive attention. It decides what may be debated, whose concerns count, and which options appear realistic. Excluding an issue from discussion can be more effective than defeating it in a vote.

### Influencing behaviour

Power alters conduct through rewards, penalties, persuasion, norms, or surveillance. "Carrots and sticks" rely on incentives and punishments, while disciplinary power creates self-regulation.

### Decision-making

Decision-making power selects a binding course of action. The lecture uses **demonetization** to show the capacity of a government to impose a national decision. Whether the policy achieved its stated goals is a separate question.

### Sovereign power

Sovereignty identifies the highest public authority within a political order. Legal supremacy does not mean unlimited practical ability. Governments remain constrained by constitutions, opposition, economic conditions, social resistance, and international pressure.

### Legitimate and illegitimate power

Power can produce an outcome without being regarded as rightful. **Legitimacy** asks whether its exercise is accepted as justified. A government may possess lawful authority but weak practical power, while another actor may impose an outcome without legitimate authority.

## How to analyze power

For any case, ask:

1. Who are the actors?
2. What relationship connects them?
3. What objective does each actor pursue?
4. What resources support them?
5. What resistance exists?
6. Which methods are used?
7. Is behaviour externally compelled or internally regulated?
8. Who sets the agenda and makes the decision?
9. What outcome occurs?
10. Is the exercise of power considered legitimate?

## Key terms

- **Power:** Capacity to realize an objective despite resistance.
- **Potential power:** Resources or authority that could affect outcomes.
- **Effective power:** Successful production of an intended result.
- **Resistance:** Conduct that opposes, evades, or obstructs another actor's objective.
- **Social relationship:** Structured interaction between actors within which power operates.
- **Coercion:** Use or threat of force or punishment.
- **Influence:** Capacity to alter beliefs or behaviour without a binding command.
- **Domination:** Stable command-and-obedience relationship.
- **Authority:** Recognized right to make binding decisions.
- **Agenda-setting:** Power to determine which issues and options receive consideration.
- **Sovereign power:** Highest public authority within a political order.
- **Disciplinary power:** Regulation through observation, examination, normalization, and training.
- **Power-knowledge:** Mutual production of knowledge systems and power relations.
- **Panopticon:** Bentham's design based on unverifiable surveillance and asymmetrical visibility.
- **Self-discipline:** Regulation of one's conduct in anticipation of observation or judgment.
- **Normalization:** Production and enforcement of standards of normal conduct.
- **Governmentality:** Techniques and knowledge used to classify and administer populations.
- **Bounded serialities:** Administrative population categories used to organize rule.
- **Legitimacy:** Belief that an exercise of power is rightful or justified.
