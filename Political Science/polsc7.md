# Power: Relations, Resistance, and Social Control

> [!summary]
> **Power** is the capacity to realize an objective even when others resist. It is relational, appears in political and social life, and does not depend solely on violence or formal office. Weber explains power through social relationships and the ability to carry out one's will despite resistance. Foucault widens the analysis by showing how surveillance, norms, and discipline lead people to regulate themselves even when no identifiable ruler is issuing commands.

> [!info] Source note
> These notes reconstruct Lecture 7 from the transcript and refined transcript. Obvious transcription errors have been corrected. Historical clarification about Bentham's panopticon has been added where the lecture compressed the example.

## Why Political Science Studies Power

Earlier lectures defined politics through collective decision-making and examined the state, government, and citizenship. Another influential approach makes **power** the central concept of politics.

From this perspective, political science asks:

- How does the state exercise power over citizens?
- How do the police, military, courts, and bureaucracy shape conduct?
- Why can some groups influence public decisions more easily than others?
- How do states exercise power over one another?
- How do families, workplaces, markets, religions, and social groups regulate behaviour?
- How do people resist, avoid, negotiate, or reproduce power?

The language of international politics reflects this focus. Terms such as **superpower**, **rising power**, and **balance of power** rank states by their ability to influence outcomes. However, political power is not confined to governments or international relations.

## Everyday Ideas of Power

The class identified several ordinary uses of the term:

- A political party is "in power" after forming a government.
- Governments make decisions that bind other people.
- Police, courts, and militaries can compel behaviour.
- Violence can be used to overcome resistance.
- Wealth can increase a person's influence.
- Employers exercise power within workplaces.
- Families and social groups shape acceptable conduct.
- Public figures influence large audiences.
- Institutions are organized through hierarchies.

These examples point to different meanings:

| Meaning | Example |
|---|---|
| **Formal position** | A party controls enough legislative seats to form a government |
| **Capacity** | The police possess legal and material means to regulate traffic |
| **Influence** | A public figure changes how followers think or act |
| **Coercion** | An institution threatens punishment for disobedience |
| **Decision-making** | An authority selects a policy that others must follow |
| **Social control** | Gender norms shape conduct without a direct command |

A useful theory of power must explain all these cases without reducing power to only office, force, or popularity.

## A Working Definition

The lecture uses the following broad definition:

> **Power is the capacity to realize an objective despite resistance or opposition.**

This definition contains four parts:

1. **An actor:** an individual, group, institution, or state capable of acting.
2. **An objective:** the result the actor seeks.
3. **Resistance:** another actor, institution, norm, or material condition that obstructs the objective.
4. **Capacity or achievement:** the ability to overcome that resistance and affect the result.

The definition is deliberately neutral about the method used. Power may operate through:

- Law
- Violence
- Threats
- Rewards
- Persuasion
- Negotiation
- Expertise
- Wealth
- Surveillance
- Social expectations

The method is the **means**. Power concerns the capacity to shape the **outcome**.

## Means, Objectives, and Outcomes

An action should not be identified as power without asking what it is intended to achieve.

Violence, for example, is a means rather than a complete definition of power. An authority may use violence to:

- Punish an individual
- Deter others
- Produce obedience
- Silence opposition
- Demonstrate control

Whether the act realizes power depends on its objective. If repression strengthens resistance instead of producing obedience, the authority used force but failed to achieve its intended outcome.

The lecture's classroom example makes this distinction. A teacher may ban devices to make students pay attention. If students comply outwardly but learn nothing, the rule has controlled visible conduct without reaching the larger goal of learning.

> [!important]
> Observing an action does not always reveal its political purpose. To evaluate power, identify the actor's objective, the method used, the resistance encountered, and the result produced.

## Capacity, Exercise, and Success

The class debated whether power exists only when an objective is achieved. Three ideas should be separated:

| Concept | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Capacity or potential power** | Possession of resources or authority that could affect others | A traffic officer has legal power to issue penalties |
| **Exercise of power** | Use of those resources in an attempt to shape conduct | The officer directs traffic or issues a fine |
| **Effective power** | Successful production of the intended result | Drivers follow traffic rules and traffic is regulated |

A government may hold office and possess constitutional authority while failing to carry out a policy. It remains the government "in power" in ordinary speech, but its effective power over that issue is weak.

Likewise, an actor may possess power and choose not to exercise it. A credible capacity can itself alter behaviour. People may comply because they know that punishment is possible, even when no punishment occurs.

## Colloquial and Analytical Meanings

When people say that a party "came to power," they usually mean that it gained the numbers and legal authority needed to form a government. This does not prove that it can achieve every objective.

| Colloquial statement | More precise interpretation |
|---|---|
| "The party is in power." | It currently forms or controls the government |
| "The police have power." | Law gives them specified authority and coercive capacity |
| "The government is powerful." | It can translate objectives into outcomes despite resistance |
| "The celebrity has power." | Their influence changes the conduct or beliefs of others |

Holding office, possessing authority, and exercising effective power overlap, but they are not identical.

## The Problem of Hidden Objectives

Power is harder to evaluate when an actor's declared objective differs from its actual objective.

Public institutions often announce goals through laws, policies, manifestos, regulations, and official statements. These sources provide evidence, but political actors may have unstated purposes. A state can justify military intervention through security or human rights while critics argue that strategic or economic interests drove the decision.

Analysts should therefore compare:

- Officially declared objectives
- Institutional incentives
- Allocation of resources
- Repeated patterns of conduct
- Who benefits and who bears the cost
- Actual outcomes

Claims about hidden motives require evidence. Failure to reach a declared objective may indicate weak power, poor policy design, conflicting objectives, or that the declaration concealed another goal.

## Power Is Relational

Power is not a substance that an actor owns in isolation. It exists in a **relationship**.

Common relationships include:

- Individual and individual
- Individual and group
- Group and group
- Institution and individual
- Institution and group
- Institution and institution
- State and citizen
- State and state

An institution is a formally organized group with recognized positions, procedures, and boundaries. A university, court, ministry, or corporation is therefore different from a loosely defined religious, cultural, class, or social group.

Power also changes across relationships. A professor may exercise power over a class but remain subject to a dean, university rules, and government law. A wealthy person may dominate an economic relationship while lacking authority in a court. No actor is simply powerful in every context.

### Relative and hierarchical power

Power is often described comparatively:

- One person influences more people than another.
- A dean supervises several academic units.
- A director possesses authority over the dean.
- A national government has powers unavailable to a municipality.

Hierarchies matter, but they do not eliminate resistance from below. Subordinates can evade rules, withhold cooperation, reinterpret instructions, organize collectively, or exploit gaps in enforcement.

## Max Weber on Power

**Max Weber** studied modern institutions, bureaucracy, rationalization, and the organization of social life. He defined power in relational terms:

> [!quote]
> Power is the probability that an actor within a social relationship can carry out their will despite resistance, regardless of the basis on which that probability rests.

The definition adds several important ideas:

1. Power exists within a **social relationship**.
2. Different actors may want incompatible outcomes.
3. Resistance is expected rather than exceptional.
4. Power concerns the chance of carrying out one's will despite that resistance.
5. Power can rest on many resources, not only law or force.

### Sources of power

Weber's account does not reduce power to a single resource. Power may be supported by:

- Wealth and control of material resources
- Knowledge and expertise
- Organizational position
- Social status and prestige
- Political office
- Religious standing
- Control of administration
- Access to coercive force

Wealth and knowledge often make actors more capable of navigating institutions and influencing decisions. Yet a resource becomes politically relevant only through the relationships in which it can affect others.

### Resistance from below

Formal hierarchy does not make subordinates powerless. Students may find ways around attendance rules, workers may slow production, citizens may evade regulations, and bureaucrats may reinterpret policy during implementation.

Resistance does not always defeat power. It shows that power is an interaction rather than a one-way transmission. Authorities anticipate resistance, change incentives, close loopholes, negotiate, or intensify enforcement. Those subject to power adapt in turn.

## Power and Nonviolent Resistance

The lecture uses nonviolence to show why coercive capacity does not guarantee effective power.

Colonial or authoritarian authorities may use violence to produce fear and submission. Nonviolent protesters refuse to supply the expected response. If repression fails to secure obedience, damages the authority's legitimacy, attracts wider support for protesters, or makes enforcement too costly, the state's violence no longer produces its intended outcome.

Nonviolent resistance therefore contests power by disrupting the relationship on which obedience depends. It can:

- Deny cooperation
- Make repression politically costly
- Expose the limits of coercion
- Attract new participants
- Change public interpretation of the conflict

The state retains weapons and formal authority, but its ability to realize its objective may decline.

## Beyond Command: Social and Structural Power

A narrow model imagines power as a visible command:

> Actor A orders Actor B to do something, and B obeys.

Many forms of power do not work this way. People modify their behaviour because they anticipate judgment, exclusion, punishment, or ridicule. No official needs to be present.

Examples include expectations about:

- Public displays of affection
- Suitable careers for women and men
- Marriage and parenthood
- Acceptable clothing
- Language and profanity
- Respectable sexuality
- Caste and religious conduct
- Behaviour on a university campus

These norms can shape action before any person issues a command. Power has become embedded in ordinary conduct.

## Michel Foucault on Power

**Michel Foucault** moved the study of power away from an exclusive focus on sovereign rulers, formal institutions, and direct commands.

For Foucault:

- Power is dispersed through social relations.
- Institutions organize conduct through routines, classification, observation, and examination.
- Power produces norms about what is normal, healthy, disciplined, or deviant.
- People internalize these norms and monitor themselves.
- Individuals are not merely passive victims. They transmit, reproduce, negotiate, and resist power.
- Wherever power operates, possibilities of resistance also arise.

This does not mean that governments or hierarchies cease to matter. It means that power also operates through schools, hospitals, prisons, workplaces, families, knowledge, and everyday habits.

## The Panopticon

The **panopticon** was a prison design proposed by **Jeremy Bentham**, not Foucault. Foucault later used it as a model of disciplinary power in *Discipline and Punish*.

Its basic design places a watchtower at the centre and cells around it. The observer can potentially see prisoners, while prisoners cannot know when they are being watched.

The political effect does not require continuous observation:

1. A prisoner knows that observation is possible.
2. The prisoner cannot verify whether anyone is watching.
3. The prisoner behaves as though surveillance is continuous.
4. External monitoring becomes self-monitoring.

> [!warning] Transcript correction
> The transcript describes open cells from which prisoners can leave and suggests that guards would shoot escapees. That is not the defining principle of Bentham's design. The key feature is **asymmetrical visibility**, which makes unverifiable surveillance an efficient means of discipline.

### Panoptic power in society

Foucault uses the panopticon to explain a wider pattern. People may regulate themselves because they might be observed or evaluated.

Examples include:

- Students behaving differently when an exam may be monitored
- Workers altering conduct when performance is continuously measured
- Residents avoiding conduct neighbours might condemn
- Users curating speech because platforms, employers, or governments may inspect it
- Citizens complying with norms even when no officer is present

The watcher becomes less important than the structure that induces self-discipline.

## Internalization and Reproduction of Power

Power persists when people internalize social expectations and reproduce them in their own conduct.

Patriarchy, for example, is not maintained only when men directly command women. Women and men may both enforce gender norms because those norms appear natural, respectable, safe, or unavoidable. Similar reproduction occurs through caste practices, standards of respectability, expectations about marriage, and rules governing speech.

This process can be summarized as:

1. A social norm defines proper conduct.
2. Institutions and communities reward conformity or punish deviation.
3. Individuals learn to anticipate these responses.
4. They regulate themselves and sometimes regulate others.
5. The norm survives without constant direction from a central authority.

Power is therefore productive as well as restrictive. It does not only prohibit actions. It shapes identities, habits, categories, and ideas about what is possible.

## Weber and Foucault Compared

| Dimension | Max Weber | Michel Foucault |
|---|---|---|
| **Main focus** | An actor's ability to carry out their will within a social relationship | Dispersed practices that shape conduct, knowledge, bodies, and norms |
| **Resistance** | Opposition that an actor may overcome | Present throughout relations of power |
| **Typical setting** | Institutions, organizations, bureaucracy, class, status, and politics | Prisons, schools, hospitals, surveillance, discipline, and everyday norms |
| **Location of power** | Actors and resources within social relationships | Networks of relations and practices, not one permanent centre |
| **Mechanism** | Carrying out one's will despite resistance | Observation, normalization, classification, examination, and self-discipline |
| **View of individuals** | Actors with objectives and resources | Both subjects of power and participants in its reproduction or resistance |

The two approaches are compatible at a basic level. Weber helps identify actors, objectives, resources, and resistance. Foucault helps explain power that lacks a single visible commander or explicit decision.

## Manifestations of Power

The lecture introduces several ways in which power becomes visible.

### Agenda-setting

Agenda-setting determines which issues, goals, and alternatives receive attention.

An actor exercises agenda-setting power when they decide:

- What counts as a public problem
- Which questions may be debated
- Which options are considered realistic
- When and where a decision will occur
- Whose concerns are excluded

Setting the agenda can be more powerful than winning a vote because excluded alternatives never reach the decision stage.

### Influencing behaviour

Power can alter conduct through rewards, penalties, persuasion, norms, or surveillance. The familiar "carrot and stick" model uses incentives and punishments, while disciplinary power produces self-regulation without constant intervention.

### Decision-making

Decision-making power is the ability to select a binding course of action. Government decisions can affect citizens even when many disagree.

The lecture cites **demonetization** as an example of a decision imposed at national scale. The example demonstrates the capacity to make a binding decision. Whether the policy achieved its stated objectives is a separate question about effective power and policy outcomes.

### Sovereign power

Sovereign power refers to a highest authority within a political order. In the modern state, sovereignty identifies a level above which no ordinary domestic authority exists.

Sovereignty does not mean unlimited practical ability. A sovereign government may face constitutional rules, political opposition, economic limits, social resistance, and international pressure. It remains legally supreme while its effective power varies.

### Legitimate and illegitimate power

Power can be judged legitimate or illegitimate. Legitimacy asks whether people regard its exercise as rightful, justified, or properly authorized.

The lecture only introduces this distinction. Authority, obligation, and legitimacy are developed in the following lectures.

> [!note]
> Power and legitimacy are different questions. An actor may successfully impose an outcome without having a recognized right to do so. Another actor may possess lawful authority but lack the practical power to secure compliance.

## A Framework for Analyzing Power

For any case, ask:

1. Who are the relevant actors?
2. What relationship connects them?
3. What objective does each actor pursue?
4. Which objectives are declared, and which may be hidden?
5. What resources or capacities does each actor possess?
6. What resistance exists?
7. Which methods are used to overcome resistance?
8. Does the actor alter behaviour, set the agenda, or make the decision?
9. Is compliance externally enforced or internally produced?
10. Who reproduces the relevant norms?
11. What outcome occurs?
12. Is the power considered legitimate?

This framework avoids treating visible force as the only evidence of power.

## Central Tensions

### Capacity versus achievement

Does an actor possess power because it could act, or only when it successfully produces the intended result?

### Declared versus actual objectives

How can power be evaluated when public reasons conceal strategic purposes?

### Violence versus power

Does violence show strength, or can repeated reliance on violence reveal an inability to secure willing compliance?

### Institution versus relationship

Is power located in an office, or does the office become powerful only through relationships of obedience, enforcement, and recognition?

### External control versus self-discipline

Must someone issue a command, or can surveillance and social norms make individuals govern themselves?

### Domination versus resistance

Can any power relationship eliminate resistance, or does resistance remain possible within every social relation?

## Exam-Ready Synthesis

1. One major tradition in political science treats politics as the study of power and its manifestations.
2. Power is broadly defined as the capacity to realize an objective despite resistance.
3. Violence, persuasion, law, rewards, surveillance, and expertise are possible means of power, not definitions of power themselves.
4. Analysis should distinguish potential power, its exercise, and its effectiveness.
5. A party may be "in power" because it forms the government without being able to achieve all its policy goals.
6. Power is relational and can connect individuals, groups, institutions, and states in different combinations.
7. Power is relative to a relationship and context. An actor powerful in one setting may be subordinate in another.
8. Weber defines power as the probability of carrying out one's will within a social relationship despite resistance.
9. Wealth, knowledge, status, office, organization, and coercive capacity can all support power.
10. Subordinates retain ways to resist, evade, reinterpret, or negotiate rules.
11. Nonviolent resistance can weaken coercive power by denying the obedience that repression seeks.
12. Foucault shows how power operates through surveillance, normalization, classification, and self-discipline.
13. Bentham designed the panopticon, while Foucault used it to explain disciplinary power.
14. Social norms can regulate behaviour without a visible authority or direct command.
15. People reproduce power when they enforce internalized norms on themselves and others.
16. Power manifests through agenda-setting, influence over behaviour, decision-making, and sovereignty.
17. Effective power and legitimate authority are distinct and must be analyzed separately.

## Key Terms

- **Power:** Capacity to realize an objective despite resistance or opposition.
- **Potential power:** Resources or authority that could affect outcomes even when not actively used.
- **Exercise of power:** Use of capacity in an attempt to shape conduct or decisions.
- **Effective power:** Successful realization of an intended outcome.
- **Resistance:** Conduct or conditions that obstruct, evade, reinterpret, or oppose another actor's objective.
- **Social relationship:** Structured interaction between actors within which power can operate.
- **Coercion:** Use or threat of force or punishment to secure conduct.
- **Influence:** Capacity to alter beliefs or behaviour without necessarily issuing a binding command.
- **Agenda-setting:** Power to determine which issues, goals, and alternatives receive consideration.
- **Decision-making power:** Capacity to select a course of action binding on others.
- **Sovereign power:** Highest public authority within a political order.
- **Disciplinary power:** Power that organizes conduct through observation, normalization, examination, and training.
- **Panopticon:** Bentham's prison design based on asymmetrical visibility, used by Foucault as a model of disciplinary surveillance.
- **Self-discipline:** Regulation of one's own conduct in anticipation of observation or judgment.
- **Internalization:** Adoption of social expectations as guides to one's own behaviour.
- **Reproduction of power:** Continuation of a power structure through routine conduct by the people within it.
- **Normalization:** Production and enforcement of standards defining normal and deviant conduct.
- **Legitimacy:** Belief or judgment that the exercise of power is rightful or justified.
- **Authority:** Recognized right to issue commands or make binding decisions.
