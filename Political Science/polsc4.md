# Citizenship: Membership, Rights, and Belonging

> [!summary]
> **Citizenship** is a legally and politically recognized relationship of membership between an individual and a state. It gives a person a status, a set of rights and duties, and a claim to political participation and belonging. Citizenship promises free and equal membership, but its rules also distinguish insiders from outsiders and often produce unequal experiences among formal citizens.

> [!info] Source notation
> Sections marked **From the book** draw directly from Anupama Roy's *Mapping Citizenship in India*. Unmarked sections primarily reconstruct the lecture and rough notes. Sections marked **Legal clarification** correct transcript errors or update legal details not covered by Roy's book.

## From the Modern State to Citizenship

The lecture begins by connecting citizenship to the modern state discussed in [[polsc3|State and Government: Features and Functions]]. The modern state need not always be secular, liberal, or democratic, but it ordinarily differs from older forms of rule by possessing impersonal **public institutions**, laws, procedures, and a permanent population.

- A religious identity does not by itself prevent an entity from being a modern state.
- A monarchy can develop modern constitutional and public institutions, as Bhutan has done.
- A theocratic monarchy concentrated in a ruler or dynasty, without accountable public institutions, is further from the modern institutional ideal.
- Stateless or anarchist communities and communes can organize collective life without a conventional state, but historically they have usually operated at a smaller scale and have often been short-lived or vulnerable to conquest.

Citizenship addresses the **population** component of statehood: which people are members of the state, on what basis, and with what consequences?

## Three Guiding Questions

1. **Who is a citizen?** What criteria establish political membership?
2. **What does citizenship provide?** Which rights, duties, protections, and benefits are specific to citizens?
3. **What is the citizen-state relationship?** What may the state demand from citizens, and what may citizens claim from the state?

Each question can be approached in two ways:

| Approach | Citizenship question | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Empirical** | Who is legally treated as a citizen, and how does the system work in practice? | Indian citizenship law establishes different rules according to date and circumstances of birth. |
| **Normative** | Who *should* be a citizen, and what should citizenship entail? | Everyone born and raised in a territory should be entitled to membership. |

An empirical description does not settle a normative debate. A law can tell us who currently qualifies without proving that its criteria are just.

## Defining Citizenship

### Citizenship as Membership

At its minimum, citizenship identifies a person as a full member of a political community. The state recognizes the individual as belonging to its permanent population, while the individual acquires claims against and obligations toward the state.

This definition contains several dimensions:

- **Legal status:** formal recognition under constitutional and statutory law
- **Rights:** enforceable claims and protected freedoms
- **Duties:** obligations such as obeying valid law and, where applicable, taxation or public service
- **Political agency:** the capacity to participate in collective self-government
- **Identity and belonging:** identification with a political community
- **Practice:** the effective ability to exercise rights and participate, not merely possess a passport

> [!important]
> Citizenship is not adequately defined as an agreement to obey laws. Most people acquire citizenship at birth without giving explicit consent, and non-citizens within a territory must also obey its laws.

### Recognition and Its Problem

Citizenship involves **state recognition**, but a purely top-down definition is dangerous. If membership exists only because an authority recognizes it, that authority might also withdraw recognition arbitrarily.

The relevant questions are therefore:

- Which public rules govern recognition?
- Are those rules constitutional, non-arbitrary, and reviewable?
- What proof can the state reasonably demand?
- Can a person challenge wrongful exclusion?
- Can citizenship be removed, and under what safeguards?

Citizenship thus links state power to constitutionalism, due process, and accountability.

### T. H. Marshall: Free and Equal Membership

> [!book] From the book
> Roy's Introduction uses T. H. Marshall's theory to explain citizenship as free and equal membership consisting of civil, political, and social elements.

In *Mapping Citizenship in India*, Anupama Roy uses **T. H. Marshall's** influential account of citizenship as **free and equal membership in a political community**. Marshall divides citizenship into three elements:

| Element | Meaning | Typical examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Civil citizenship** | Rights necessary for individual freedom | Personal liberty, speech, conscience, property, access to justice |
| **Political citizenship** | Participation in the exercise of political power | Voting, contesting elections, holding public office |
| **Social citizenship** | Minimum welfare, security, and access to society's shared heritage | Education, healthcare, social security, dignified living conditions |

Citizenship therefore exceeds legal nationality. It concerns whether people can participate in political life and live as equal members of society.

## Citizens, Persons, Residents, and Visitors

The state's relationship with an individual exists along a spectrum rather than as a simple citizen/non-citizen binary.

| Status | Character | Political position |
|---|---|---|
| **Citizen** | Full legal member of the state | Normally possesses the complete set of citizen-specific political rights |
| **Permanent resident** | Authorized to live indefinitely in the state | Usually has many civil and social rights but restricted political rights |
| **Temporary resident** | Authorized to live for a limited purpose or period | Rights depend on visa or residence conditions |
| **Visitor** | Present for a short, authorized stay | Protected as a person but has few membership claims |
| **Refugee/asylum seeker** | Seeks protection from persecution or danger | Possesses human rights; legal status and durable membership may remain unsettled |
| **Stateless person** | Not recognized as a national by any state under its law | Especially vulnerable because no state accepts full responsibility for political membership |

Residence does not automatically become citizenship. Some states permit long-term or permanent residence without guaranteeing eventual naturalization. Conversely, a citizen can continue to hold membership while residing abroad.

## Human Rights and Citizenship Rights

Citizenship is not the source of every right.

### Human or Personhood Rights

**Human rights** attach to people because they are human, not because they are citizens. Following the Second World War and the **Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948)**, states increasingly accepted obligations toward people within their jurisdiction regardless of nationality.

Examples include protection of life, liberty, bodily integrity, and due process. A foreigner does not become rightless upon crossing a border.

### Citizen-Specific Rights

Some rights follow specifically from membership in a political community. The clearest example is the **right to vote**, which permits citizens to participate in making the authority to which they are subject.

In India, constitutional rights are distributed through different formulations:

- Some provisions protect **all persons**, such as Article 14's equality before the law and Article 21's protection of life and personal liberty.
- Some provisions are expressly restricted to **citizens**, including freedoms under Article 19 and equality of opportunity in public employment under Article 16.
- Voting in national and state elections is a statutory political right ordinarily limited to adult citizens.

> [!note]
> Rights are not simply "granted" at the state's pleasure. In a constitutional order, many rights limit state action and can ground claims against the government itself.

## What Makes Someone a Citizen?

The class tested several possible criteria. Each captures something relevant but can become exclusionary if treated as the sole basis of membership.

| Proposed criterion | Appeal | Main difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| **Obedience to law** | Connects membership with duty | Non-citizens obey laws too; infants never consented |
| **State recognition** | Reflects legal status | Risks circularity and arbitrary withdrawal unless recognition follows public rules |
| **Economic contribution** | Connects benefits with contribution | Excludes children, disabled people, unpaid caregivers, the unemployed, and others; visitors also contribute economically |
| **Land ownership** | Historically linked citizenship to a durable stake in the community | Privileges wealth and excludes tenants, workers, and the landless |
| **Voting or public service** | Connects citizenship with participation | Circular because citizenship is often required to vote or hold office; minors remain citizens |
| **Military service/allegiance** | Demonstrates commitment to the state | Makes membership militaristic and excludes those unable or unwilling to fight |
| **Dependence on state resources** | Recognizes that everyone relies on public order and infrastructure | Dependence is nearly universal and does not distinguish citizens from residents |
| **Emotional attachment** | Recognizes identity and belonging | Difficult to verify and insufficient as a legal rule |
| **Choice at adulthood** | Values consent and autonomy | Children need a secure status from birth; renunciation may create statelessness |

The strongest general criteria discussed are **birth in the territory**, **descent from citizens**, and **a durable connection formed by residence**. Modern legal systems commonly combine them.

## Main Routes to Citizenship

### Jus Soli: Right of the Soil

**Jus soli** grants citizenship because a person is born within the state's territory.

- It treats territorial birth as a sufficient connection to the political community.
- It reduces inherited exclusion and is strongly associated with countries such as the United States.
- A qualified version may require that the child or parents also have lawful or durable residence.

### Jus Sanguinis: Right of Blood

**Jus sanguinis** grants citizenship through descent from one or more citizen parents.

- It preserves membership across generations and across borders.
- Taken alone, it is circular: each generation's status depends on the previous generation's citizenship.
- Strict descent rules can exclude children born and socialized in a state while extending membership to descendants who have never lived there.

### Naturalization

**Naturalization** is the acquisition of citizenship after birth, usually through an application based on residence and other statutory conditions. States may require language competence, knowledge of institutions, an oath of allegiance, or renunciation of a previous nationality.

### Registration and Incorporation of Territory

- **Registration** provides a simplified statutory route for specified groups, such as certain persons of Indian origin, spouses, or minor children of citizens.
- **Incorporation of territory** makes designated inhabitants citizens when a territory becomes part of the state.

### Citizenship by Investment

Some states grant citizenship in exchange for substantial investment. This exposes a normative tension: if citizenship represents equal political membership, should wealth purchase faster access to it?

## Permanence, Choice, and Multiple Membership

Citizenship raises difficult questions about time and consent:

- Is citizenship permanent or can it expire?
- Should long residence create a right to naturalize?
- Can citizenship be earned through participation or attachment?
- May a person renounce citizenship freely?
- Should a state permit **dual citizenship**?
- What safeguards are needed to prevent **statelessness**?

Citizenship may be acquired or renounced under law, but it is not an ordinary club membership. Its loss can remove political rights, security of residence, and access to state protection. Deprivation therefore demands much stronger safeguards than cancellation of a private membership.

India does not recognize full dual citizenship. **Overseas Citizenship of India (OCI)** is a special immigration and affiliation status, not constitutional citizenship: OCI cardholders do not receive rights such as voting or eligibility for many public offices.

## Citizenship in India

### Constitutional Commencement: Articles 5-11

> [!book] From the book
> Roy's Chapter 1 explains Articles 5-11 through Partition, migration, displacement, and the transition from British subjecthood to Indian citizenship.

The Constitution does not give a single abstract definition of "citizen." **Part II (Articles 5-11)** primarily determined who was a citizen at the Constitution's commencement in the exceptional context of Independence and Partition.

- **Article 5:** citizenship based on domicile plus birth, parentage, or ordinary residence
- **Article 6:** citizenship of certain people who migrated from Pakistan to India
- **Article 7:** treatment of certain people who migrated to Pakistan but later returned under permits
- **Article 8:** citizenship of certain persons of Indian origin residing abroad
- **Article 9:** voluntary acquisition of foreign citizenship prevents citizenship under Articles 5, 6, or 8
- **Article 10:** continuance of citizenship subject to parliamentary law
- **Article 11:** Parliament may regulate acquisition and termination of citizenship

Roy argues that these provisions must be read through **Partition**, mass migration, displacement, return, and the effort to draw legal boundaries around the new Republic. The shift from British subjecthood to citizenship promised equal self-rule, while the new border simultaneously forced institutions to decide who belonged to India and who belonged to Pakistan.

### Citizenship Act, 1955

> [!book] From the book
> The five routes to citizenship and the Act's relationship with Article 11 are summarized from Chapter 1.

Using Article 11, Parliament enacted the **Citizenship Act, 1955**, which provides principal routes through:

1. Birth
2. Descent
3. Registration
4. Naturalization
5. Incorporation of territory

The Act also regulates renunciation, termination, and deprivation of citizenship.

### Changing Rules for Citizenship by Birth

> [!book] From the book and legal clarification
> Roy traces the movement from broad birthright citizenship toward descent-based restrictions through the 1986 and 2003 amendments. The exact effective date ranges below are stated explicitly for clarity.

India moved from broad territorial birthright toward increasingly qualified birthright citizenship:

| Date of birth in India | General rule under Section 3 of the Citizenship Act |
|---|---|
| **26 January 1950 to 30 June 1987** | Citizen by birth, subject to limited statutory exceptions |
| **1 July 1987 to 2 December 2004** | At least one parent must have been an Indian citizen at the time of birth |
| **On or after 3 December 2004** | Both parents must be citizens, or one must be a citizen and the other must not be an illegal migrant at the time of birth |

This progression marks a shift from **jus soli** toward a stronger **jus sanguinis** or descent-based model. The 1992 amendment separately made citizenship by descent available through either parent rather than only the father, removing a gender-discriminatory rule.

### Assam Accord and the 1986 Amendment

> [!book] From the book
> Roy's Chapter 2 treats Assam as a politics of place-making in which the migrant became a suspect or constitutive outsider. The lecture introduces the same tension between citizenship and migration.

The Assam movement raised questions about migration, electoral membership, Assamese identity, and the detection of foreigners. The **Assam Accord (1985)** and **Section 6A**, inserted into the Citizenship Act in 1985, created special rules according to dates of entry into Assam. The 1986 amendment then narrowed citizenship by birth nationwide by requiring at least one citizen parent for people born from 1 July 1987 onward.

Roy's broader point is that the migrant increasingly changed from a person seeking passage into citizenship after Partition to a category associated with suspicion and illegality. Citizenship law thus became part of a politics of **place-making**: defining the legitimate people of a territory by identifying outsiders.

### Citizenship Amendment Act and NRC

> [!warning] Legal clarification
> Roy's book predates the Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 and the completed Assam NRC. This section clarifies claims made in the lecture and is not drawn from the book.

The **Citizenship (Amendment) Act, 2019 (CAA)** was enacted, not merely proposed. It creates an expedited route to citizenship for specified Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi, and Christian migrants from Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan who entered India on or before 31 December 2014, subject to the Act's conditions. Its religious classification prompted major constitutional and political controversy.

The **National Register of Citizens (NRC)** is a register intended to identify Indian citizens. An NRC exercise was completed in Assam in 2019 under its distinct historical and legal framework. A nationwide NRC has been discussed politically but has not been implemented. The CAA and NRC are legally distinct, though public debate often connects them because documentation and classification determine who is recognized, excluded, or potentially offered another route to citizenship.

> [!warning] Analytical distinction
> Neither the CAA nor an NRC is itself a complete new definition replacing all prior Indian citizenship law. They must be situated within the Constitution, the Citizenship Act, its amendments, administrative procedures, and judicial review.

## Partition, Borders, and the Problem of Belonging

Partition demonstrates that citizenship is not an abstract label. A newly drawn border can transform neighbours into citizens, refugees, migrants, foreigners, or stateless persons. Bureaucratic decisions about documents and intention then have life-changing consequences.

The literary example mentioned in class is Saadat Hasan Manto's **"Toba Tek Singh,"** whose central character is **Bishan Singh**. During an exchange of asylum inmates between India and Pakistan, he cannot make sense of which country contains his home and dies in the no-man's-land between the two. The story exposes the absurdity and violence of forcing complex identities into newly fixed territorial categories.

The example remains relevant when long-term residents are declared foreigners or pushed toward a border despite decades of social membership. Such cases separate:

- **Formal legal status** from lived belonging
- **Administrative recognition** from social reality
- **Territorial borders** from memories, families, and everyday life
- **Proof of citizenship** from citizenship itself

## Anupama Roy: Citizenship's Central Paradox

> [!book] From the book
> This entire section summarizes the main conceptual argument of Roy's Introduction, including momentum, encompassment, closure, differentiated citizenship, constitutive outsiders, and residual citizens.

Roy presents citizenship as simultaneously inclusive and exclusive.

### Citizenship as a Momentum Concept

A **momentum concept** contains an internal push toward wider equality and inclusion. Citizenship expands when excluded groups struggle to become recognized as equal members. Feminist movements, Indigenous peoples, environmental movements, workers, minorities, and anti-colonial movements have all used the language of rights and citizenship to make claims.

Citizenship's promise has two parts:

- **Horizontal equality:** citizens relate as formal equals rather than through inherited rank.
- **Integration:** previously excluded groups enter an expanding political community.

### Encompassment and Closure

- **Encompassment** is citizenship's movement toward including more people and recognizing difference within a democratic universal framework.
- **Closure** is the simultaneous drawing of limits that deny, defer, or condition membership.

Every rule saying who belongs also helps define who does not. The outsider is therefore **constitutive** of citizenship: the category of citizen gains meaning partly through contrast with the alien, foreigner, or illegal migrant.

### Formal Equality and Substantive Inequality

The liberal citizen is often imagined as an abstract, unmarked bearer of equal rights. This can be emancipatory because caste, class, gender, race, or religion should not determine legal worth. However, ignoring those differences can conceal unequal capacities to exercise rights.

For example, a right to vote is formally equal, but a migrant worker without a stable address may be absent from the electoral roll. Formal membership remains intact while effective political citizenship is weakened.

### Differentiated Citizenship

**Differentiated citizenship** recognizes that equal treatment may require attention to historically disadvantaged groups rather than identical rules for everyone.

Indian constitutional examples include:

- Special provisions for socially and educationally backward classes, Scheduled Castes, and Scheduled Tribes under Article 15
- Reservations in public employment under Article 16
- Abolition of untouchability under Article 17
- Cultural and educational rights of minorities under Articles 29 and 30
- Directive Principles seeking social welfare and reduction of inequalities

This model attempts to combine individual equality with recognition of group disadvantage. It also creates a warning: group protection must not permit internal domination or make the rights of women and other members conditional.

### Residual Citizens

Roy calls many migrant workers **residual citizens**: people who possess formal citizenship but are treated as marginal populations in practice.

- Welfare systems often require fixed residence and documents.
- Electoral registration depends on an identifiable address.
- Informal workers may lack social security and collective bargaining.
- Slum clearances and repeated relocation disrupt access to work, voting, education, and services.

Internal migrants therefore show that a person can be a legal citizen and still experience **disenfranchisement, dispossession, and disempowerment**. Citizenship has graded lived forms even where legal status is nominally equal.

## Citizen and State: A Reciprocal but Unequal Relationship

The citizen-state relationship is reciprocal in principle:

| Citizen's claims on the state | State's claims on the citizen |
|---|---|
| Recognition and secure membership | Compliance with constitutionally valid law |
| Protection of civil and human rights | Taxes and other lawful obligations |
| Political participation and representation | Respect for the equal rights of others |
| Access to justice and accountable institutions | Civic responsibility and, in some systems, public service |
| Social conditions necessary for meaningful freedom | Allegiance within the limits of constitutional morality |

The relationship is nevertheless structurally unequal because the state controls law, documents, borders, coercion, and official recognition. Constitutional rights and judicial review are essential because they turn the citizen from a passive **subject** into a claimant who can question public power.

The transition is never complete. As Gianfranco Poggi argues, modern states formally address people as citizens but often continue to administer them as subjects or populations to be counted, categorized, commanded, and surveilled.

## Core Tensions

1. **Status versus practice:** Is citizenship a passport or the effective capacity to act as an equal member?
2. **Rights versus duties:** Which is foundational, and can duties be demanded without rights?
3. **Territory versus descent:** Should birth and residence matter more than ancestry?
4. **Consent versus inheritance:** Most citizenship is assigned at birth, yet political theory values voluntary membership.
5. **Permanence versus mobility:** Migration unsettles the assumption that one person belongs permanently to one bounded state.
6. **Universalism versus exclusion:** Citizenship promises equality but creates legal outsiders.
7. **Individual versus community:** Equal individual rights may require recognition of group histories and disadvantages.
8. **Formal versus substantive equality:** Identical legal rights do not guarantee equal ability to exercise them.
9. **State recognition versus self-identification:** Personal belonging alone does not create legal status, but bureaucracy can also deny genuine social membership.
10. **National citizenship versus human rights:** Human dignity is universal, while many political rights remain territorially bounded.

## Exam-Ready Synthesis

1. Citizenship is a legal-political relationship of membership connecting an individual to a state through status, rights, duties, participation, and belonging.
2. Normative analysis asks who should belong; empirical analysis asks who the law and institutions actually recognize.
3. Obedience, economic contribution, land, voting, or emotional attachment cannot individually provide an adequate universal rule of citizenship.
4. The principal bases of citizenship are territorial birth, descent, registration, naturalization, and incorporation of territory.
5. Human rights protect persons generally, whereas political rights such as voting are ordinarily citizen-specific.
6. Residence and citizenship are distinct statuses; long-term presence need not produce political membership.
7. India's constitutional citizenship provisions arose from Independence, Partition, migration, and state formation.
8. The Citizenship Act, 1955 regulates citizenship after the Constitution's commencement, and later amendments have narrowed citizenship by birth.
9. Roy describes citizenship as both a momentum toward equality and a mechanism of closure that constructs outsiders.
10. Formal legal equality may coexist with unequal lived citizenship structured by caste, class, gender, religion, migration, and access to documents.
11. Internal migrant workers can become residual citizens: formally members but practically excluded from welfare, stable residence, and political participation.
12. Citizenship remains an evolving and contested relationship rather than a fixed or purely administrative category.

## Key Terms

- **Citizenship:** Legally and politically recognized membership in a state, carrying status, rights, duties, participation, and belonging.
- **Citizen:** A full legal member of a political community.
- **Nationality:** Legal affiliation to a state in international and domestic law; often used interchangeably with citizenship, though citizenship emphasizes political membership and participation.
- **Normative:** Concerned with what ought to be.
- **Empirical:** Concerned with what exists and how it operates in practice.
- **Recognition:** Formal acceptance by a state that an individual is one of its members.
- **Personhood rights:** Rights held by all persons regardless of citizenship.
- **Political rights:** Rights to participate in public power, especially voting and eligibility for office.
- **Civil rights:** Rights protecting individual liberty, legal capacity, and access to justice.
- **Social rights:** Claims to welfare, security, and conditions necessary for dignified social membership.
- **Jus soli:** Citizenship based on birth within a territory, or "right of the soil."
- **Jus sanguinis:** Citizenship based on descent from citizen parents, or "right of blood."
- **Naturalization:** Acquisition of citizenship after birth through a statutory application process.
- **Domicile:** A person's legally recognized permanent home, combining residence with an intention to remain; it is not identical to temporary residence.
- **Permanent resident:** A non-citizen authorized to reside indefinitely in a state.
- **Dual citizenship:** Simultaneous citizenship of two states.
- **OCI:** Overseas Citizenship of India, a special status for eligible foreign nationals of Indian origin; it is not full Indian citizenship.
- **Statelessness:** The condition of not being considered a national by any state under its law.
- **Constitutive outsider:** An excluded figure through contrast with whom the citizen and the limits of membership are defined.
- **Momentum concept:** A concept whose internal logic pushes continually toward wider freedom, equality, and inclusion.
- **Encompassment:** Expansion of citizenship through inclusion and democratic recognition of difference.
- **Closure:** Legal or political restriction, denial, or deferral of membership.
- **Differentiated citizenship:** Equality pursued through recognition and accommodation of group-specific histories or disadvantages.
- **Residual citizen:** A formal citizen who is practically marginalized from political, social, or economic citizenship.
- **Disenfranchisement:** Loss or denial of the effective right to vote or participate politically.
- **Governmentality:** Practices through which states classify, enumerate, supervise, and administer populations.
