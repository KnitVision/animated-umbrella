# Introduction to Political Science

> [!summary]
> Political science studies how people organize collective life, exercise and contest power, govern, cooperate, and manage conflict. It does not accept familiar terms such as *people*, *government*, *policy*, or *power* at face value; it investigates how they are defined, who is included, and how political arrangements operate in practice.

## What Is Politics?

A preliminary definition of politics is:

> The activity through which people collectively decide how to govern themselves, what policies to adopt, and how to manage cooperation and conflict.

This definition is useful but incomplete because every term within it raises further questions:

- Who counts as **the people**?
- What makes someone a **person** or a **citizen**?
- What does it mean to **govern**?
- Is governance distinct from control or domination?
- What is a **policy**, and how is one created and implemented?
- What is **power**, and how can it be observed or measured?
- Whose opinions influence political decisions?
- How do cooperation and conflict shape collective outcomes?

Political science is therefore a **discipline of inquiry**. Its task is not merely to use political terms but to investigate and clarify them.

## Political Categories Are Contested

Terms such as *people*, *person*, and *citizen* are not fixed or politically neutral. Institutions and laws determine who is recognized and who is excluded.

### The meaning of "the people"

Political communities have historically restricted participation according to:

- Age
- Gender
- Property ownership
- Taxpayer status
- Legal status or citizenship

Ancient Greek democracy, for example, is remembered as an early democracy, but political participation was limited to a narrow group rather than the whole population. Similarly, changing voting ages show that the legal meaning of political membership changes over time.

### The meaning of "person"

Whether an entity is legally recognized as a person can become a political question. Law does not merely reflect a settled definition; political processes help create, revise, and contest that definition.

> [!important]
> Defining a political category distributes rights, duties, recognition, and power. Asking who counts is therefore itself part of politics.

## Core Questions of Political Science

Political science examines several connected dimensions of collective life.

### Ideas and Concepts

It clarifies concepts that are commonly used without precision, such as:

- Democracy
- Freedom
- Equality
- Globalization
- Citizenship
- Power
- Authority
- Legitimacy
- Obligation

For example, calling the present world "globalized" immediately raises questions about globalization's defining features and how they can be identified.

### Political Actors

Political decisions can be studied at several levels:

- **Individuals:** Why does a person vote, protest, or remain politically inactive?
- **Groups and collectives:** Why do social groups develop particular political preferences?
- **Political parties:** Why do parties adopt particular agendas?
- **Institutions:** How do courts, legislatures, and bureaucracies exercise authority?
- **States:** How do states govern internally and interact with one another?

Choosing not to participate is also political behavior. Political science can ask why a campus, community, or social group remains largely inactive even when political participation is possible.

### Institutions

Political institutions are durable structures that perform public functions. Important examples include:

- Courts
- Legislatures
- Executives
- Bureaucracies
- Electoral bodies
- Political parties

Political science studies their design, authority, behavior, effectiveness, and relationships.

### Processes

Politics occurs through processes rather than isolated events. Examples include:

- Elections
- Government formation
- Lawmaking
- Policy formulation
- Policy implementation
- Political mobilization
- Protest

A law does not simply appear: actors propose it, institutions debate it, authorities approve it, administrators implement it, and citizens may comply with or contest it.

### Scales of Analysis

Political inquiry can operate at different scales:

| Scale | Examples |
|---|---|
| Campus or local | Student political activity, municipal governance |
| City | Comparing governance in Mumbai, Delhi, Hyderabad, or Patna |
| Subnational | Maharashtra politics versus Bihar politics |
| National | Political institutions and behavior in India, Pakistan, or France |
| International | Relations and interactions among states |

## Forms of Government

Political science studies how governments are organized and whether different models achieve their stated purposes.

Normative debate may ask whether democracy, dictatorship, centralized authority, or decentralized rule is preferable. Empirical inquiry asks:

- How is the model actually structured?
- How does it operate?
- Under what conditions is it stable?
- Who benefits or loses?
- Does it achieve its intended outcomes?

## Normative and Empirical Political Science

The distinction between **normative** and **empirical** inquiry is foundational.

### Normative Approach

Normative political science concerns values, principles, ideals, and what political life **ought to be**.

Typical questions include:

- What is a good society?
- What is the best form of government?
- Who should participate in politics?
- How should power be distributed?
- What should equality or freedom mean?
- Which values should institutions prioritize?

A normative model begins with a value. For example, one system might prioritize efficiency, while another prioritizes low resource use. Neither claim initially describes an existing system; each proposes a principle that should guide design.

Political equivalents include:

- Centralized authority may be valued for unity or decisiveness.
- Decentralized authority may be valued for participation or autonomy.
- Universal suffrage may be defended as a requirement of equality.

### Empirical Approach

Empirical political science studies observable forms, behavior, institutions, processes, and outcomes.

Typical questions include:

- Is a society equal in practice?
- How free are citizens in a particular country?
- Which electoral system is used?
- How do people actually vote?
- How does a bureaucracy implement policy?
- Under what conditions do democracies become unstable?

Empirical research translates broad ideas into forms that can be observed, compared, measured, or evaluated.

### Relationship Between Them

| Normative | Empirical |
|---|---|
| Asks what *ought* to be | Asks what *is* and how it works |
| Focuses on values and principles | Focuses on evidence and observable outcomes |
| Develops ideals and possibilities | Studies institutions, behavior, and cases |
| Example: society should be equal | Example: measure whether inequality exists |
| Example: everyone should have a vote | Example: determine who legally votes and through which electoral system |

The two approaches are complementary:

1. Normative theory identifies political goals and principles.
2. Empirical analysis examines how those principles take institutional form.
3. Evidence reveals whether institutions realize their stated goals.
4. Those findings can lead to revised principles or institutional reforms.

The spread of democracy during decolonization illustrates this relationship. Centuries of democratic thought supplied normative ideas, but constitution-makers in newly independent states still had to choose concrete electoral, legislative, and constitutional arrangements.

## Major Subfields of Political Science

### Political Philosophy

Political philosophy is most directly concerned with normative questions. It examines ideals such as:

- Justice
- Freedom
- Equality
- Rights
- The good society
- Legitimate government

Its central concern is how political life should be organized and which principles should guide it.

### Political Theory

Political theory historically shared political philosophy's normative orientation. During the twentieth century, especially in American political science, it became increasingly concerned with theories that could be evaluated using evidence.

Political theory may therefore:

- Formulate general explanations of political behavior or institutions.
- Identify conditions expected to produce particular outcomes.
- Test those expectations against historical or quantitative evidence.
- Connect abstract concepts to observable political patterns.

The lecture uses **Maurice Duverger** as an example of theory-building about party systems: recurring patterns across multiparty democracies can be proposed as a theory and then evaluated as more cases become available.

> [!note] Philosophy versus theory
> In the lecture's distinction, political philosophy remains primarily concerned with ideals, while modern political theory often attempts to make broader claims empirically testable. In actual academic usage, the boundary can overlap and vary by institution.

### Comparative Politics

Comparative politics studies political similarities and differences across cases.

Examples include:

- Comparing the post-independence trajectories of India and Pakistan
- Comparing elections or party systems across countries
- Asking why direct or electronic participation may work differently in small and large populations
- Comparing governance across cities or states

Comparison helps distinguish features unique to one case from broader political patterns.

### Political Behavior

Political behavior studies why individuals and groups make political choices.

Questions include:

- Why do people vote for a particular party?
- Why do voting patterns differ by gender, religion, caste, or class?
- Why do people protest?
- Why do some people avoid political participation?
- How do media and social networks influence political preferences?

**Psephology**, the systematic study of elections and voting, is one part of this field.

### Public Administration

Public administration examines how bureaucracies organize and deliver public functions. It concerns administration of the public realm rather than only direct interaction with "the people."

Examples include decisions about:

- Roads, traffic, and flyovers
- Water and electricity supply
- Healthcare
- Tree removal and urban development
- Transport and other public services

The central question is how the administrative machinery functions and translates decisions into services, even when it does so imperfectly.

### Public Policy

Public policy studies how rules, laws, constitutions, and governance decisions are designed and implemented.

It asks:

- What problem is the policy intended to solve?
- Which groups does its language recognize?
- On what principles are its rules based?
- How is it implemented?
- Do practical outcomes match the policy's stated goals?

### International Relations

International relations studies how countries interact. Its subjects include cooperation, conflict, diplomacy, security, and the behavior of states within the international system.

## The Empirical Turn

Twentieth-century political science, particularly in the United States, increasingly emphasized measurement and prediction. Researchers attempted to model politics by identifying conditions associated with outcomes such as voting behavior, party competition, or regime change.

### Promise of Empirical Analysis

- Makes broad claims testable
- Reveals patterns across large numbers of cases
- Allows systematic comparison
- Helps evaluate whether a theory matches observable reality
- Can support evidence-based policy analysis

### Limits and Risks

- Human behavior may be reduced to overly simple numerical relationships.
- Historical, cultural, ideological, and colonial contexts may be ignored.
- Correlation may be treated as a complete explanation.
- Models can create an unjustified impression of certainty.
- Political data can be used to manipulate rather than merely understand behavior.

The lecture presents **Francis Fukuyama's** *The End of History and the Last Man* as an example of a broad claim that liberal democracy had become the dominant endpoint of ideological development after the Cold War. Subsequent political developments demonstrate the danger of treating contingent historical trends as final outcomes.

### Cambridge Analytica

The Cambridge Analytica controversy illustrates a harmful use of political empiricism:

1. Platforms gather large amounts of behavioral data.
2. Analysts infer political preferences or psychological traits.
3. Campaigns target users with selected content.
4. Data analysis shifts from explaining behavior to attempting to influence it.

This raises questions about privacy, consent, manipulation, democratic autonomy, and the ethics of computational political research.

## Computational Political Science

Computational methods can support political inquiry by analyzing large bodies of information, including:

- Voting and electoral data
- Public-policy documents
- Survey responses
- Social-media activity
- Patterns of policy implementation
- Demographic and administrative data

For example, a researcher can combine textual analysis of a health or welfare policy with quantitative evidence about its implementation among rural women or other intended beneficiaries.

> [!warning]
> Computation does not remove the need for political interpretation. Researchers must still decide what to measure, which categories to use, whose data is represented, and what ethical limits apply.

## Power and Influence

Power is broader than direct physical coercion.

- **Coercive power:** arresting, punishing, or physically harming someone
- **Administrative power:** making and implementing decisions that shape people's options
- **Influence:** altering behavior without issuing a direct command
- **Surveillance effect:** people regulate themselves because they believe they may be watched

Influence is therefore a form of power. A person may behave differently not because force is currently being used, but because authority, observation, or possible consequences shape their choices.

## Humans as Political Animals

Calling human beings **political animals** does not mean that everyone votes, joins a party, or attends protests. It means that people live within relationships of power, authority, rules, collective decisions, and disagreement.

- Political participation is behavior to be explained.
- Political non-participation is also behavior to be explained.
- Expressing an opinion about authority is political.
- Debating whether an opinion may be expressed is itself political.

The state remains relevant even to non-voters because laws, institutions, and public administration continue to structure their lives.

## Protest and Political Expression

Protest raises several political-science questions:

- Who is considered entitled to protest?
- Must a person be directly affected to join a movement?
- How do organizers differ from the wider movement they initiate?
- Why do some people support a grievance but reject its mode of expression?
- How do political traditions shape acceptable forms of dissent?
- How does social media mobilize, polarize, or fragment participants?
- Can "the youth" be treated as one political category despite internal differences?

### Movement versus Organization

A movement should not automatically be reduced to the organization that begins or coordinates it. Participants may support the broader cause while disagreeing with a particular organizer, strategy, or demand.

### Diversity Within Political Categories

Statements such as "the youth are angry" conceal differences within the group. Rigorous analysis must ask:

- Which young people?
- Angry about what?
- How is this attitude measured?
- Are participants and non-participants being distinguished?
- What social, regional, economic, or ideological divisions exist?

### Social Media and Polarization

Social media has become both a site and a process of politics. It can:

- Spread information and mobilize participants
- Expose users to competing positions
- Create polarized or filtered information environments
- Merge judgments about organizers with judgments about a movement
- Shape perceptions of how widespread a political opinion is

## Central Place of State and Citizenship

The **state** and the **citizen** are the course's two foundational concepts.

- Discussions of "the people" often concern citizenship.
- Discussions of government, public power, and political order concern the state.
- Citizens relate to the state through rights, duties, authority, legitimacy, and obligation.
- Even those who avoid formal politics remain subject to state institutions and rules.

These concepts connect to the course's later themes:

- State and government
- Citizen and citizenship
- Power and authority
- Equality
- Legitimacy
- Political obligation
- Political ideas and ideologies
- Public policy
- Political actors

## Exam-Ready Synthesis

1. Political science critically investigates collective decision-making, governance, power, cooperation, and conflict.
2. Political categories such as *people*, *person*, and *citizen* are historically variable and politically contested.
3. Normative inquiry asks what political arrangements ought to be; empirical inquiry studies how they actually operate.
4. Political science studies ideas, actors, institutions, processes, and multiple scales of political activity.
5. Its major subfields include political philosophy, political theory, comparative politics, political behavior, public administration, public policy, and international relations.
6. Empirical and computational methods can reveal patterns but can also oversimplify or manipulate political behavior.
7. Power includes coercion, administration, influence, and the capacity to shape behavior through anticipated surveillance.
8. Participation, non-participation, protest, and political expression are all subjects of political inquiry.
9. State and citizenship are foundational because political life is structured through their relationship.

## Key Terms

- **Politics:** Collective activity involving governance, power, cooperation, conflict, and public decisions.
- **Political science:** Systematic inquiry into political ideas, actors, institutions, behavior, and processes.
- **Normative:** Concerned with values, principles, and what ought to be.
- **Empirical:** Concerned with observable evidence, behavior, institutions, and outcomes.
- **Political philosophy:** Study of political ideals and principles.
- **Political theory:** Development of explanations and frameworks for political concepts, behavior, and institutions.
- **Comparative politics:** Systematic comparison of political cases.
- **Political behavior:** Study of political choices by individuals and groups.
- **Psephology:** Study of elections and voting behavior.
- **Public administration:** Study of bureaucracy and the delivery of public functions.
- **Public policy:** Study of the creation, content, implementation, and effects of public decisions.
- **International relations:** Study of interactions among states and other international actors.
- **Political actor:** An individual, group, organization, or institution capable of shaping political outcomes.
- **Power:** Capacity to shape another actor's behavior or available choices.
