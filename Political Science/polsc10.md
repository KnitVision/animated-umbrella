# Political obligation, conscience, and civil disobedience

> [!summary]
> **Political obligation** does not require blind obedience. Henry David Thoreau argues that when the state makes a person an agent of serious injustice, conscience may require refusal. **Civil disobedience** makes that refusal public and directs it at political authority. It differs from ordinary lawbreaking because it challenges the legitimacy of a law or policy and accepts the risk of punishment to expose the conflict between legality and justice.

> [!info] Sources and corrections
> The main discussion comes from Lecture 10 and continues [[polsc9|Authority, Legitimacy, and Political Obligation]]. Thoreau's claims were checked against *Resistance to Civil Government*, usually published as *Civil Disobedience*. The lecture's references to a "wall" concern Thoreau's opposition to the Mexican-American War, not a physical border wall. The tax at issue was a poll tax, and Thoreau's refusal was also tied to slavery.

## The problem left by political obligation

The previous lectures separated power, authority, legitimacy, and obligation:

| Concept | Question |
|---|---|
| **Power** | Can the state secure compliance? |
| **Authority** | Does the state have a recognized or justified right to command? |
| **Legitimacy** | Is its rule, law, or action accepted or justified as rightful? |
| **Political obligation** | Does a person have a legal or moral duty to obey? |

Lecture 10 asks what happens when these answers diverge. A state may possess the legal power to tax and wage war while using those powers unjustly. A command may be lawful without generating a moral duty to obey it.

> [!important]
> Thoreau does not deny that the government has power. He disputes its moral claim on him when his compliance would help sustain injustice.

This distinction prevents two mistakes:

- Disobedience does not require pretending that the state is powerless.
- Recognition of the state does not require approving every law or policy.

## Thoreau's historical setting

Thoreau wrote his essay after refusing to pay a poll tax and spending one night in jail. His protest responded to two connected injustices.

### Slavery

Slavery remained lawful in much of the United States. The state protected a system that treated human beings as property and implicated taxpayers, officials, and ordinary citizens in its continuation.

### The Mexican-American War

Thoreau opposed the war of 1846-48. He saw it as territorial expansion that could extend slaveholding power. His objection was therefore not to taxation in the abstract. He objected to material support for a government pursuing slavery and conquest.

The episode illustrates a recurring problem: routine acts can connect citizens to policies they did not choose personally. Paying a tax appears administratively ordinary, but revenue supports the state's actions as a whole.

## Law and justice can conflict

Law claims authority, but legal validity does not guarantee justice. The state can enact and enforce rules that violate equality, liberty, or human dignity.

| Question | Possible answer |
|---|---|
| Is the rule legally valid? | Yes |
| Can the state enforce it? | Yes |
| Is it morally justified? | No |
| Must a person obey it? | Disputed |

This is the same distinction made in [[polsc8|Power, Authority, Legitimacy, and Obligation]] and [[polsc9|Authority, Legitimacy, and Political Obligation]]. **Legality**, **legitimacy**, and **justice** overlap, but none automatically proves the others.

Thoreau sharpens the point. If obeying a law makes a person an agent of injustice against someone else, obedience is no longer morally innocent. The issue is not merely whether a person dislikes the law. It is whether their action helps produce or maintain serious harm.

## Conscience as a limit on obedience

For Thoreau, the individual's first moral responsibility is to do what they judge right. Citizenship does not transfer this responsibility to the state.

Conscience involves:

- Recognizing that a public action has moral consequences
- Judging whether those consequences are right or wrong
- Accepting personal responsibility for one's contribution
- Refusing to hide behind law, office, custom, or majority opinion

This claim is demanding. It says that a person cannot answer every moral objection with "I followed the rules" or "the government decided." Political institutions shape circumstances, but individuals still act within them.

### Conscience is not preference

Conscientious refusal is not the same as ignoring any inconvenient rule. Thoreau's claim must be read in the context of injustice to others.

| Preference-based violation | Conscientious refusal |
|---|---|
| Avoids a rule for convenience or private advantage | Refuses complicity in a judged injustice |
| Need not communicate any political claim | Seeks to expose a conflict between law and justice |
| Treats punishment as a cost to escape | May accept punishment as part of the protest |
| Usually concerns the actor's benefit | Often concerns harm done to others |

A driver who ignores a traffic rule to save time is disobedient in the ordinary sense. A person who openly violates a segregation law to challenge its injustice makes a political and moral claim.

## Majority rule is not moral truth

Democratic government often uses majority decision-making. Thoreau rejects the inference that a majority must therefore be right.

Majorities may prevail because they are:

- More numerous
- Better organized
- Socially dominant
- Politically represented
- Able to use the state's institutions

None of these facts proves moral correctness. A majority can support slavery, segregation, war, persecution, or censorship. Democratic procedure matters, but conscience cannot be replaced by counting votes.

> [!warning]
> Thoreau's position is not a rejection of every collective decision. It denies that numerical victory settles whether a decision is just.

### A tension in Thoreau's view

If each individual treats personal conscience as final, people may reach incompatible judgments. Conscience can also be shaped by prejudice, propaganda, religion, or ideology. The lecture therefore raises a hard question: how can a person know that conscience is morally reliable?

Thoreau does not provide a complete public procedure for resolving this disagreement. His argument works most forcefully as a refusal of moral passivity. It does not by itself supply a full theory of democratic decision-making.

## From disagreement to civil disobedience

A person can respond to a disputed state action at several levels:

1. **Inattention:** remain unaware or decide that the issue does not matter.
2. **Moral judgment:** notice the action and decide whether it is right or wrong.
3. **Dissent:** communicate disagreement through speech, writing, petitions, or legal challenge.
4. **Non-cooperation or civil disobedience:** refuse participation and make that refusal politically visible.
5. **Revolution:** seek to replace the political order rather than correct one law or policy.

These stages are analytically distinct. Moral concern does not guarantee action, and disobedience does not necessarily aim at revolution.

### What makes disobedience civil?

In the lecture's account, civil disobedience has several features:

- It responds to a perceived injustice.
- It deliberately breaches or refuses a legal requirement.
- It communicates a reason to the state and usually to fellow citizens.
- It challenges the legitimacy of a law, policy, or use of power.
- It is undertaken as a matter of conscience rather than private gain.
- It often accepts legal consequences rather than concealing the breach.

Civil disobedience is therefore both an act and an argument. The breach interrupts normal compliance. Its visibility tells the state why compliance has been withdrawn.

## Why visibility matters

Quiet non-compliance can avoid complicity, but public non-compliance adds a political purpose. It places a claim before the state and the political community.

Thoreau's imprisonment mattered even though it lasted only one night and did not end either slavery or the war. The action showed that the government's demand had encountered principled refusal.

The lecture connects this logic to Gandhi's Salt March. Coastal residents could make salt without a public march, but the march transformed a private breach into a collective challenge to colonial taxation and authority. The point was not simply to obtain salt. It was to make the injustice of the law visible and reproducible as resistance.

> [!important]
> The immediate policy outcome is not the only measure of civil disobedience. An act may expose injustice, create a public record, encourage others, or weaken the appearance of unanimous consent even when the law remains in force.

## Taxation, routine compliance, and complicity

Taxation illustrates how political obligation becomes habitual. Most citizens do not reconsider the whole state each time they pay. The state collects revenue through an ordinary administrative process, and compliance becomes routine.

Thoreau interrupts that routine. His refusal asks:

- What does my money enable?
- Does regular payment imply consent?
- Has the state sought continuing authorization for its policies?
- At what point does contribution become complicity?

The social-contract tradition often imagines consent as the basis of government. Thoreau's challenge is that a one-time or tacit agreement cannot excuse every later act of the state. Legitimate authority must remain answerable to those it governs.

### Obligation must be maintained

Political obligation is not a blank cheque. Even if a state is legitimate overall, particular actions can lose moral authority. Citizens must therefore continue to evaluate what public institutions do in their name and with their resources.

This produces a conditional relationship:

`legitimate institutions` → `presumptive duty to obey` → `serious injustice` → `reassessment` → `possible refusal`

The duty is **presumptive**, not absolute. It ordinarily counts in favour of obedience, but grave injustice may defeat it.

## Responsibility under authority

The lecture uses the "superior orders" defence associated with the post-war trials of Nazi officials to make a broader point. Acting under authority does not erase individual responsibility.

Authority can:

- Issue commands
- Threaten punishment
- Define official roles
- Normalize conduct
- Reward compliance

Yet the actor still participates in the resulting deed. "I was ordered to do it" may explain conduct without fully justifying it.

### Arendt and the banality of evil

Hannah Arendt's phrase **the banality of evil** describes how grave wrongdoing can be carried out through ordinary administration, routine obedience, and failures of judgment. It does not mean that evil is minor. It warns that perpetrators need not appear monstrous; they may treat destructive actions as normal work.

This reinforces Thoreau's concern about routine compliance. Bureaucratic normality can distance a person from the consequences of their acts.

## Structure, ideology, and personal agency

The strongest objection raised in the lecture is that conscience is not formed in isolation. States, families, schools, religions, media, and political ideologies shape what people regard as normal or right.

This connects to [[polsc7|Power: Relations, Resistance, and Social Control]]:

- **Disciplinary power** trains people to regulate themselves.
- **Power-knowledge** helps define normality and deviance.
- **Ideology** can organize beliefs and present one social order as natural.
- **Group pressure** can punish dissent without a formal state command.

If power shapes moral judgment, an appeal to conscience cannot guarantee a just result. A committed racist may describe segregation as conscientious. A nationalist may regard violence as duty.

### The lecture's response

The lecture does not deny structural influence. It rejects the stronger claim that influence removes all agency. Individuals can experience doubt, compare principles with consequences, listen to those harmed, and refuse participation.

The practical cost may be severe. A dissenter can face arrest, exclusion, financial loss, or violence. Failure to act may be understandable under coercion, but practical danger and moral rightness remain different questions.

| Question | Concern |
|---|---|
| What is right? | Moral judgment |
| What action is possible? | Capacity and risk |
| What action is likely to work? | Strategy and consequences |
| What am I responsible for? | Agency and complicity |

Conflating these questions makes moral judgment depend entirely on predicted success. Thoreau's point is that a person can have a duty not to cooperate even when one refusal will not change state policy.

## Civil disobedience and revolution

Civil disobedience normally challenges a law, policy, or practice while addressing the existing political community. Revolution seeks a more complete replacement of institutions or authority.

| Civil disobedience | Revolution |
|---|---|
| Limited or focused refusal | Transformation of the political order |
| Communicates an injustice | Denies the regime's continuing claim to rule |
| May appeal to existing constitutional values | May propose a new constitutional foundation |
| Can coexist with broad recognition of the state | Withdraws legitimacy from the system as a whole |

Thoreau allows that accumulated injustice may eventually make revolution necessary. His tax refusal, however, is an individual withdrawal of cooperation. It is meant to place friction against the machinery of injustice.

## Evaluating a claim of civil disobedience

Use the following questions:

1. Which law, policy, or state action is being challenged?
2. What injustice does the dissenter identify?
3. How does compliance contribute to that injustice?
4. Is the breach public and communicative?
5. Is it directed at political authority or merely undertaken for private benefit?
6. Have ordinary channels of dissent been attempted or blocked?
7. What risks fall on the dissenter, bystanders, and those already harmed?
8. Does the action seek reform, non-cooperation, or revolution?
9. Is the appeal to conscience supported by reasons others can assess?
10. What would count as success beyond immediate policy change?

## Key terms

- **Political obligation:** Legal or moral duty to obey political authority.
- **Presumptive obligation:** A duty that normally applies but can be outweighed by stronger reasons.
- **Conscience:** Individual moral judgment about right action and personal responsibility.
- **Complicity:** Contribution to or participation in another actor's wrongdoing.
- **Civil disobedience:** Deliberate, politically communicative breach or refusal of law undertaken to protest injustice.
- **Non-cooperation:** Withdrawal of assistance, participation, or compliance from an authority or system.
- **Dissent:** Expression of disagreement with an authoritative decision or dominant view.
- **Majoritarianism:** Treating majority preference as sufficient authority, often without adequate protection for rights or minorities.
- **Legality:** Conformity with valid law.
- **Legitimacy:** Accepted or justified rightfulness of authority or its exercise.
- **Superior orders:** Defence that an actor committed a deed because an authority commanded it.
- **Banality of evil:** Arendt's account of grave wrongdoing performed through ordinary routines and failures of judgment.
- **Revolution:** Attempt to replace the basic political order or its ruling authority.
