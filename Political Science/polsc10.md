# Political obligation, conscience, and civil disobedience

> [!summary]
> Thoreau accepts that the state can tax, punish, and wage war. His question is whether those powers always deserve obedience. When compliance makes someone an agent of injustice, he gives conscience priority over law. Civil disobedience turns that private judgment into a public refusal addressed to the state.

> [!info] Sources and corrections
> This note reconstructs Lecture 10 and continues [[polsc9|Authority, Legitimacy, and Political Obligation]]. Thoreau's claims were checked against *Resistance to Civil Government*, now usually published as *Civil Disobedience*. The unclear reference to a "wall" in the transcript concerns his opposition to the Mexican-American War, not a physical border wall. He refused a poll tax in protest against slavery and the war.

## Where the argument begins

The last lecture separated four questions:

| Concept | What it asks |
|---|---|
| Power | Can the state make people comply? |
| Authority | Does it have the right to command? |
| Legitimacy | Is its rule or action rightful? |
| Political obligation | Must a person obey? |

These questions can produce different answers. The American government had the power and legal authority to collect Thoreau's tax. He denied that this created a moral duty to fund slavery and territorial war.

That is the dispute. Thoreau is not pretending the government lacks power. He is refusing its moral claim on him.

## Slavery, war, and a poll tax

Thoreau spent one night in jail after refusing to pay a poll tax. His protest grew out of two connected events.

Slavery still had legal protection in much of the United States. The Mexican-American War of 1846-48 also raised the prospect that slavery would spread into conquered territory. Thoreau believed that paying the tax would help a government engaged in both injustices.

His objection was not "taxes are annoying." It was a claim about complicity. An ordinary payment entered a state treasury that financed policies he considered gravely wrong.

## Law is not the same as justice

The lecture returns often to one distinction:

> A law can be valid and enforceable without being just.

Segregation and slavery make the point obvious in hindsight. Both were once protected by law. Their legality did not settle their legitimacy, and it did not absolve every person who helped enforce them.

This continues the argument in [[polsc8|Power, Authority, Legitimacy, and Obligation]]. Law tells us what public institutions recognize and enforce. Justice asks whether that arrangement can be defended. Political obligation asks what a person should do when the two come apart.

Thoreau's answer is severe. If a law requires you to become an agent of injustice to another person, break with it.

## Conscience and responsibility

Thoreau writes that the only obligation he may rightfully assume is "to do at any time what I think right." Read alone, the sentence sounds like permission to do whatever one wants. The rest of the essay rules out that reading.

He is discussing responsibility for harm, not taste or convenience. Conscience requires a person to:

- Notice what the state is doing
- Judge whether it is right
- Examine their own part in it
- Refuse to hide behind law or custom

A driver who breaks a traffic rule to save two minutes has not made a conscientious political protest. A person who openly violates a segregation rule to challenge its injustice has. The difference lies in the reason, the target, and the attempt to make the injustice visible.

> [!important]
> Authority can explain why someone acted. It does not automatically justify the act.

## A majority can be wrong

Democracy gives majorities a way to decide public questions. Thoreau refuses to turn that procedure into a moral test.

Majorities usually prevail because they have more votes and greater institutional strength. Neither fact proves that they are right. A majority can support slavery, persecution, or an unjust war. Conscience cannot be outsourced to a head count.

This leaves a real difficulty. Different people make different claims of conscience, and some of those claims rest on prejudice. Thoreau gives no complete procedure for settling such conflicts. His argument is strongest as an attack on passive obedience, not as a complete theory of democracy.

## What makes disobedience civil?

People can oppose the state in several ways. They may criticize a policy, challenge it in court, petition officials, refuse cooperation, or try to replace the regime. Civil disobedience occupies a specific place among these responses.

It usually has these features:

- A deliberate breach or refusal of law
- A reason grounded in public injustice
- A message directed to the state and fellow citizens
- An attempt to change a law, policy, or political practice
- Willingness to face the legal risk of refusal

Ordinary lawbreaking tries to escape notice. Civil disobedience needs an audience. The action says that the law has lost its moral claim even though the state can still enforce it.

### Why visibility matters

Thoreau's night in jail did not end the war or abolish slavery. That does not make the refusal meaningless. It broke the appearance of unanimous consent and showed that at least one citizen would not cooperate quietly.

The lecture compares this to Gandhi's Salt March. People living near the sea did not need a march to learn how to make salt. The long, public act turned salt-making into a challenge to colonial taxation. It invited others to repeat the breach and forced the government to answer it.

Civil disobedience is therefore an act with an argument inside it.

## Routine obedience and complicity

Most political obedience is mundane. A tax collector arrives, money changes hands, and the state disappears from view until the next administrative encounter. People may dislike a war or policy while continuing to support the machinery that carries it out.

Thoreau interrupts this habit. His refusal asks what the payment enables and whether routine compliance has become tacit approval.

The social-contract tradition treats consent as a basis of government. Thoreau points to the weakness in any one-time version of that consent. A state cannot claim permanent moral permission for every future act simply because citizens once accepted its authority or continue to live within its territory.

Political obligation has to survive what the state actually does.

## "I was only following orders"

The class connects Thoreau to the superior-orders defence associated with the trials of Nazi officials after the Second World War. A subordinate says that responsibility belongs to the authority who issued the command.

Orders, threats, and institutional roles matter. They can narrow a person's choices and make refusal dangerous. But they do not turn the person into an object. The subordinate still participates in the act and may understand its consequences.

Hannah Arendt's phrase **the banality of evil** belongs here. It describes grave wrongdoing carried out through paperwork, routine, and failures of judgment. The point is not that evil is trivial. It is that ordinary officials can do terrible things while treating obedience as a normal day's work.

That is also why the tax example matters. The machinery of injustice depends on many acts that look harmless when viewed one by one.

## Does power shape conscience?

The hardest objection in the lecture is that conscience does not arise outside society. Families, schools, religions, states, and ideologies teach people what to regard as normal. A person may sincerely defend an unjust order.

This objection draws on the account of discipline and normalization in [[polsc7|Power: Relations, Resistance, and Social Control]]. If power helps form judgment, telling everyone to "follow your conscience" cannot guarantee justice.

The lecture does not solve this problem. It makes a narrower claim. Social influence does not erase all agency. People can feel doubt, listen to those being harmed, compare official claims with consequences, and refuse a role assigned to them.

There is a cost. Dissenters may lose work, money, freedom, or safety. The lecture's contemporary mob example was meant to strip away an easy assumption that the right action is also the safe or effective one.

## Right action and effective action

Much of the class debate turns on outcomes. Why protest if one act will not change the policy? Why intervene if the mob will return tomorrow?

Thoreau separates moral responsibility from guaranteed success. One person refusing a tax will not stop a war. That person can still decide not to help finance it.

Four questions should remain separate:

1. Is the action right?
2. Is it possible under the circumstances?
3. Is it likely to work?
4. What happens if I comply instead?

Strategy matters, especially when an intervention may endanger others. Yet a demand for certain success can become an excuse for doing nothing. Political action rarely comes with that certainty.

## Civil disobedience and revolution

Civil disobedience normally attacks a law or policy while continuing to address the existing political community. Revolution withdraws legitimacy from the basic order and seeks to replace it.

Thoreau allows that accumulated injustice may produce revolution. His own tax refusal had a narrower form. It placed friction against the state's machinery and invited others to reconsider their cooperation.

## Exam-ready points

1. Political obligation is a claimed legal or moral duty to obey political authority.
2. State power and legal authority do not prove that a particular command is just.
3. Thoreau refused a poll tax because he opposed slavery and the Mexican-American War.
4. His central concern is complicity in injustice, not a general dislike of taxation.
5. Conscience cannot be replaced by law, office, or majority opinion.
6. Majority rule is a decision procedure, not proof of moral truth.
7. Civil disobedience is deliberate, public, political, and directed against injustice.
8. Its visibility distinguishes it from concealed lawbreaking.
9. The Salt March turned a common illegal act into a challenge to colonial authority.
10. Superior orders may explain conduct without excusing it.
11. Arendt's banality of evil shows how routine obedience can carry grave harm.
12. Social power shapes conscience, but it does not always eliminate reflection or refusal.
13. Moral rightness, practical risk, and political effectiveness are different questions.
14. Civil disobedience seeks limited change, while revolution seeks a new political order.

## Key terms

- **Political obligation.** A legal or moral duty to obey political authority.
- **Conscience.** A person's judgment about right action and responsibility.
- **Complicity.** Participation in or material support for another's wrongdoing.
- **Civil disobedience.** A deliberate and public breach of law meant to protest injustice.
- **Non-cooperation.** Withdrawal of assistance or compliance from an authority.
- **Majoritarianism.** Treatment of majority preference as sufficient justification for rule.
- **Legality.** Conformity with valid law.
- **Legitimacy.** The accepted or justified rightfulness of rule.
- **Superior orders.** The claim that an authoritative command excuses the subordinate's act.
- **Banality of evil.** Arendt's account of grave wrongdoing carried out through ordinary routines and failed judgment.
- **Revolution.** An attempt to replace the basic political order.
