# Power, authority, legitimacy, and obligation

> [!summary]
> **Power** is the capacity to shape conduct or secure an outcome despite resistance. **Authority** is a claimed or recognized right to rule. **Legitimacy** concerns whether that claim or a particular use of power is accepted or justified. **Political obligation** concerns whether subjects have a duty to obey. The four concepts answer different questions and must not be treated as synonyms.

> [!info] Sources and examples
> The conceptual sequence comes from Lecture 8 and continues [[polsc7|Power: Relations, Resistance, and Social Control]]. Definitions were checked against Max Weber's account of legitimate domination, Jay Steinmetz's open textbook *Politics, Power, and Purpose*, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries on authority and political legitimacy. The examples below come from the open textbook or are new applications. Classroom examples have been omitted.

## The conceptual map

Suppose actor A issues a direction to person B.

| Concept | Question |
|---|---|
| **Power** | Can A make B comply or otherwise affect the outcome? |
| **Authority** | Does A have the right to issue this direction? |
| **Legitimacy** | Is A's rule or this particular direction accepted or justified as rightful? |
| **Obligation** | Does B have a duty to comply? |

These questions can produce different answers. A hacker controlling a hospital's computer system has power but no authority. A public-health officer may have authority to order an evacuation but lack the personnel needed to enforce it. A legislature may enact a valid law whose fairness remains disputed. A citizen may recognize the state yet deny a duty to obey one gravely unjust command.

> [!important]
> Compliance proves that power worked. It does not by itself prove authority, legitimacy, or obligation.

## Power

The lecture retains the definition developed in [[polsc7]]:

> **Power is the capacity to realize an objective despite resistance or opposition.**

Power is relational. It exists when the resources of one actor can affect another actor's conduct or the outcome of a shared conflict.

### Power may rest on

- Physical force or the threat of force
- Law and institutional office
- Wealth and control of resources
- Knowledge and technical expertise
- Organization and control of information
- Persuasion, reputation, or social pressure
- Surveillance and classification

Having a resource is not the same as using it successfully. Analysis should separate:

1. **Potential power**, the resources or position an actor could use.
2. **Exercise of power**, the actual attempt to shape conduct.
3. **Effective power**, success in producing the intended result.

### Book example: pilot and hijacker

**[Book: Steinmetz]** A pilot and a hijacker can both alter passengers' conduct aboard an aircraft. Passengers follow the pilot because the pilot occupies an authorized role governed by aviation rules. They follow the hijacker because of coercion. Both possess power in the descriptive sense, but only the pilot normally possesses authority.

The example shows why authority cannot mean merely "the ability to make someone obey."

## Authority

The lecture's working definition is:

> **Authority is the right to hold, command, or exercise power.**

In Weber's sociology, authority is **legitimate domination**, a stable command-and-obedience relation that followers regard as valid. Here "legitimate" first describes a belief held by followers. It does not automatically mean morally good, democratic, or just.

Contemporary political philosophy separates several claims contained in authority:

- **Liberty to rule:** the ruler is permitted to issue and enforce directives.
- **Normative power:** the ruler can change people's duties through valid directives.
- **Claim-right to rule:** the ruler is entitled to rule, and others have corresponding duties not to obstruct that rule.

These distinctions explain why authority is more than influence. Advice may give someone a reason to act. A valid directive claims to alter what that person is required to do.

### New example: contaminated water

A city's chief water engineer discovers dangerous contamination.

- Her laboratory knowledge gives her **epistemic authority** about the contamination.
- A statute may give the municipal commissioner **practical legal authority** to close the supply.
- The engineer's warning influences belief. The commissioner's order changes what residents and officials are required to do.
- If the commissioner lacks tankers to distribute safe water, the office retains authority but has weak practical power.

This case separates expertise, legal office, and effective capacity.

## Sources of authority

Authority can rest on different grounds:

- Constitutional or statutory rules
- A formally defined office
- Long-established custom
- Recognition of exceptional leadership
- Professional expertise
- Delegation or consent

The source determines the authority's scope. A marine biologist's expertise does not authorize them to sentence offenders. A judge's legal office does not make the judge an authority on bridge design.

Lecture 9 develops Weber's traditional, charismatic, and legal-rational sources in [[polsc9|Authority, Legitimacy, and Political Obligation]].

## Power without authority

Power without authority exists when an actor can secure compliance but lacks a recognized or justified right to command.

### New example: control of a housing app

A private technician discovers that they can remotely disable every smart lock in an apartment complex. The technician can force residents to follow demands by threatening a lockout. Control of the software creates power. It creates no right to govern residents.

This is **de facto control** without **de jure authority**.

## Authority without effective power

An actor may have the right to act but lack the means to secure the intended result.

### New example: election commission

An election commission validly orders a platform to stop displaying a prohibited campaign advertisement. The commission has legal authority within its jurisdiction. If the platform has no local office and ignores the order, authority exists but effective power is weak.

Authority is a normative or institutional status. Power concerns practical capacity and effects.

## Sovereign power

**Sovereignty** is the highest public authority within a political order. Its meaning changes with the level of analysis.

### State sovereignty

Externally, sovereignty means that a state is not legally subordinate to another state. Internally, it concerns final public authority over law and coercion within a territory.

### Constitutional sovereignty

In a constitutional system, officials do not rule through unlimited personal power. A constitution:

- Creates public offices
- Allocates their powers
- Sets limits on those powers
- Establishes procedures for making and reviewing law
- Provides methods for amendment

The Constitution is therefore prior to the offices it creates. A minister cannot enlarge their jurisdiction merely by claiming to represent the state.

### Popular sovereignty

**Popular sovereignty** holds that ultimate political authority originates with the people. It does not mean each individual may use any means to impose a political preference.

The relationship is reciprocal:

1. The people authorize a constitutional order.
2. The Constitution creates institutions and gives citizens political rights.
3. Citizens normally exercise power through elections, petitions, courts, public reasoning, and amendment.
4. Those procedures preserve the constitutional order while allowing change.

### Constituent and constituted power

- **Constituent power** is the authority to create or replace a constitution.
- **Constituted power** is authority exercised by offices created under that constitution.

### New example: an island federation

Three islands adopt a federal charter after a public convention and referendum. The founding convention exercises constituent power. The later federal council has only constituted power. If the charter reserves fisheries to island governments, the federal council cannot lawfully take that power merely because it commands a larger coast guard.

The example separates legal competence from coercive capacity.

## Different answers to "who really rules?"

The legal answer does not exhaust the political question.

| Approach | Main focus |
|---|---|
| **Constitutional** | Which institution has the legally final authority? |
| **Weberian** | On what belief in legitimacy does stable domination rest? |
| **Marxist** | Which class controls productive resources and shapes the state? |
| **Foucauldian** | How do institutions, knowledge, norms, and surveillance organize conduct? |
| **International relations** | Which states can constrain the choices of other states? |

These approaches may identify different holders of power because they study different relationships.

## Legitimacy

> **Legitimacy is the accepted or justified rightfulness of political authority or of a particular exercise of power.**

Two uses must be distinguished:

- **Descriptive legitimacy:** people believe that a ruler, institution, or decision is rightful.
- **Normative legitimacy:** the ruler, institution, or decision meets a defensible standard of rightfulness.

A regime may have high descriptive legitimacy among its supporters while failing a normative test based on equal rights. Conversely, a justified emergency measure may initially face distrust and possess weak descriptive legitimacy.

### Legality is not legitimacy

- **Legality** asks whether an action follows valid law.
- **Legitimacy** asks whether rule or coercion is accepted or justified as rightful.
- **Justice** asks whether institutions distribute rights, opportunities, benefits, and burdens fairly.

The ideas overlap but remain distinct. A policy can be legally enacted yet unjust. Some theorists also hold that a political order may be minimally legitimate without being fully just.

### New example: automated benefit suspension

A welfare department uses a lawfully purchased algorithm to suspend payments whenever it detects an identity mismatch. The department has authority to investigate fraud, and its procurement may be legal. If the system gives no reasons, has a high error rate, and offers no appeal, individual suspensions may still be illegitimate.

The problem is not the existence of public authority. It is the procedure and manner in which that authority is used.

## Recognition and consent

Recognition helps turn unstable coercion into durable authority. People may obey because they accept:

- A rule's legal source
- A custom
- A leader's qualities
- A decision-making procedure
- The public benefits produced by coordination

Recognition is evidence of descriptive legitimacy, not final proof of normative legitimacy. Fear, misinformation, dependency, or exclusion can produce apparent acceptance.

Consent also requires care. Silence counts as valid tacit consent only when people understand that silence signifies agreement, can object, and do not face prohibitive costs for refusing.

## Political obligation

> **Political obligation is a moral or legal duty to obey the state or another political authority.**

Political obligation asks why an authoritative directive should bind a person even when that person disagrees with its content.

### Legal and moral obligation

- A **legal obligation** exists because a valid rule requires conduct and institutions may enforce it.
- A **moral obligation** exists because independent moral reasons support the conduct.

The two can coincide or conflict.

### New example: wildfire evacuation

Officials order a mountain settlement to evacuate before a fast-moving wildfire.

- Emergency law creates a legal obligation.
- The duty not to endanger rescue workers and neighbours creates a moral reason to comply.
- The officials' access to fire data gives them relevant expertise.
- A clear explanation and equal transport assistance strengthen legitimacy.

If officials secretly exclude one hamlet from transport because its residents are politically unpopular, their office remains lawful but that discriminatory use of authority becomes harder to justify.

## Compliance, obedience, and obligation

- **Compliance** means acting as directed, whatever the motive.
- **Obedience** means acting because an authority issued the direction.
- **Obligation** means having a duty to act as directed.

A person may comply from fear without believing that obedience is owed. A person may believe a law creates an obligation yet accidentally violate it. Another may comply for independent moral reasons rather than because the state commanded it.

## Why obey?

Major justifications include:

1. **Consent:** people expressly or tacitly authorize rule.
2. **Fair play:** beneficiaries of a cooperative scheme should carry a fair share of its burdens.
3. **Public benefit:** authority provides security, order, and goods that require coordination.
4. **Natural duty of justice:** people should support reasonably just institutions.
5. **Associative duty:** membership in a political community creates special responsibilities.
6. **Expert coordination:** following a competent authority may help people act on reasons that already apply to them.

No theory gives every government an unlimited right to obedience. Each must explain the conditions and limits of the duty it claims.

## Democracy and bounded authority

Democracy does not remove power or political obligation. It subjects them to public limits.

Democratic authority normally requires:

- Publicly identifiable sources of power
- Jurisdictional limits
- Reasons for coercive decisions
- Equal application of rules
- Review and appeal
- Accountability for misuse
- Opportunities for participation and dissent
- Lawful methods of replacing rulers and changing rules

Authority becomes arbitrary when its holder cannot identify the source, scope, or review procedure for a command.

## Case-analysis checklist

For any political conflict, ask:

1. Who acts upon whom?
2. What outcome does the actor seek?
3. What resources create power?
4. Is the attempt effective?
5. What right to rule is claimed?
6. What is the source and scope of that authority?
7. Is the authority de jure, de facto, or both?
8. Do subjects accept the authority?
9. Is that acceptance free and informed?
10. Is the action legal?
11. Is it legitimate under a normative standard?
12. Does the subject have a legal or moral obligation to obey?
13. What procedures permit challenge or review?

## Exam-ready synthesis

1. Power, authority, legitimacy, and obligation answer different political questions.
2. Power is the capacity to realize an objective despite resistance.
3. Potential power, its exercise, and its effectiveness must be separated.
4. Authority is a right to rule, command, or exercise power.
5. Weber treats authority as domination accepted as legitimate by followers.
6. Descriptive acceptance does not prove moral rightfulness.
7. Power may exist without authority, and authority may exist without effective power.
8. Sovereignty is the highest public authority within a political order.
9. Constitutional sovereignty limits officials to powers assigned by the Constitution.
10. Popular sovereignty locates the ultimate source of political authority in the people.
11. Constituent power creates a constitutional order; constituted institutions operate within it.
12. Descriptive legitimacy concerns belief; normative legitimacy concerns justification.
13. Legality, legitimacy, and justice overlap but are not identical.
14. Political obligation is the legal or moral duty to obey political authority.
15. Compliance, obedience, and obligation must be distinguished.
16. Consent, fair play, public benefit, justice, association, and coordination offer competing grounds for obligation.
17. Democratic authority requires limits, reasons, accountability, and avenues of challenge.

## Key terms

- **Power:** Capacity to shape conduct or secure an objective despite resistance.
- **Potential power:** Resources or position that could affect an outcome.
- **Effective power:** Successful production of the intended result.
- **Authority:** Claimed or recognized right to rule and issue binding directives.
- **Legitimate domination:** Weber's term for stable domination accepted as valid by followers.
- **Practical authority:** Authority to give reasons for action or issue directives.
- **Epistemic authority:** Credibility grounded in knowledge or expertise.
- **De jure authority:** Authority held by law.
- **De facto authority:** Authority exercised or generally obeyed in practice.
- **Legitimacy:** Accepted or justified rightfulness of authority or coercive power.
- **Descriptive legitimacy:** Actual belief that a ruler or institution has a right to rule.
- **Normative legitimacy:** Rightfulness judged by a defensible standard.
- **Legality:** Conformity with valid law.
- **Political obligation:** Legal or moral duty to obey political authority.
- **Compliance:** Acting as directed, regardless of motive.
- **Obedience:** Acting because an authority issued the directive.
- **Sovereignty:** Highest authority in a political order.
- **Popular sovereignty:** Principle that political authority ultimately comes from the people.
- **Constituent power:** Power to create or replace a constitution.
- **Constituted power:** Authority of institutions created by a constitution.
- **Tacit consent:** Consent inferred from conduct or silence under conditions that make refusal meaningful.
