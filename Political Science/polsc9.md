# Authority, legitimacy, and political obligation

> [!summary]
> **Authority** is a right to exercise power, not simply the ability to compel. Weber identifies three ideal types according to the source of legitimacy: **traditional**, **charismatic**, and **legal-rational authority**. Authority may also be **de jure**, grounded in law, or **de facto**, exercised in practice. **Legitimacy** can describe actual acceptance or a justified right to rule. **Political obligation** asks whether people have a legal or moral duty to obey.

> [!info] Sources and examples
> The sequence and required concepts come from Lecture 9. Definitions were checked against Weber's theory of legitimate domination, the INFLIBNET political sociology textbook chapter on domination and authority, Jay Steinmetz's open textbook *Politics, Power, and Purpose*, and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries on authority and political legitimacy. Examples below are book-based or newly created. Classroom examples have been omitted.

## From power to authority

Power asks whether an actor can affect conduct or outcomes. Authority asks whether that actor has the right to do so.

> **Authority is the recognized or justified right to issue and enforce binding directives.**

This definition contains four elements:

1. An actor or office claims a right to command.
2. The claim applies within a limited domain or jurisdiction.
3. Subjects treat the directive as providing a reason for action.
4. A source of legitimacy supports the claim.

Authority is more stable than coercion because subjects do not need to be threatened afresh each time. They follow the office, rule, custom, or leader they regard as entitled to command.

### Authority as legitimate domination

For Weber, **domination** is the probability that a command will be obeyed. **Authority** is domination supported by a belief in its legitimacy.

Here, legitimacy has a descriptive sociological meaning. Followers believe the command is valid. Weber is identifying the belief that sustains obedience, not certifying the command as morally right.

### Power and authority compared

| Power | Authority |
|---|---|
| Capacity to secure an outcome | Right to issue a binding directive |
| May rely entirely on force | Relies on a legitimacy claim |
| Can exist without willing recognition | Normally involves acceptance of a rule, office, custom, or person |
| Measured through capacity and effect | Evaluated through source, scope, and justification |

### New example: research laboratory

A senior technician knows how to shut down every experiment by cutting the power supply. That capacity gives the technician power. The laboratory safety officer is authorized by published rules to suspend an experiment when toxic gas is detected. The officer possesses authority within that safety domain.

If the safety officer orders researchers to change the topic of an unrelated paper, the command falls outside the office's jurisdiction. A person may possess authority for one purpose and lack it for another.

## Weber's three ideal types of authority

Weber classifies authority by the belief that makes rule appear legitimate.

> [!important]
> The three forms are **ideal types**. They isolate distinct principles for analysis. Real institutions usually mix them.

| Type | Basis of legitimacy | Object of obedience | Main weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Traditional** | Sanctity of inherited customs | A customary ruler or established order | Vague limits and resistance to change |
| **Charismatic** | Devotion to a person's exceptional qualities or mission | The leader personally | Instability and succession |
| **Legal-rational** | Belief in enacted rules and valid appointment | The impersonal office and legal order | Bureaucratic rigidity or formally valid abuse |

## Traditional authority

> **Traditional authority rests on belief in the sanctity of long-established customs and the right of persons designated by those customs to rule.**

People obey because "this is how authority has always been organized." Office and person are often difficult to separate.

### Common forms

**[Book: INFLIBNET]**

- **Gerontocracy:** elders rule because age carries customary standing.
- **Patriarchalism:** authority belongs to the head of a household or lineage.
- **Patrimonialism:** administration is treated as an extension of the ruler's household.
- **Hereditary succession:** office passes according to lineage rules.

### Features

- Authority rests on precedent and inherited status.
- Rules are often customary rather than codified.
- Loyalty is owed personally to a traditional ruler.
- Jurisdiction may be imprecise.
- Innovation must be presented as compatible with tradition.

### New example: customary irrigation council

Several villages share an old canal. By custom, the oldest members of hereditary water-managing families decide the order in which fields receive water. Farmers obey even though no current statute created the council.

The council's authority is traditional. A government engineer may possess legal-rational authority over dam safety, creating overlapping jurisdictions.

### Strength and weakness

Traditional authority can make expectations predictable and connect decisions to community memory. It may also preserve exclusion when age, lineage, caste, or gender determines who may rule.

## Charismatic authority

> **Charismatic authority rests on followers' devotion to the exceptional sanctity, heroism, exemplary character, or mission of an individual leader.**

Charisma is not simply charm. It exists politically only when followers recognize the person's extraordinary claim.

### Features

- Authority attaches to a person rather than an inherited office or impersonal rule.
- It often emerges during crisis or dissatisfaction with established institutions.
- Followers accept departures from convention because they trust the leader's mission.
- The leader must repeatedly demonstrate success or exceptional qualities.
- Authority weakens if followers withdraw recognition.

### New example: flood-rescue organizer

After formal relief agencies fail during repeated floods, a local radio operator coordinates boats, food, and medical help. Volunteers begin following the operator's decisions in later emergencies despite the absence of an official appointment.

The operator's authority is charismatic if obedience rests on faith in unusual courage and competence. If the district later creates a permanent disaster office and appoints the organizer under written rules, the basis begins to shift toward legal-rational authority.

### Routinization of charisma

**Routinization of charisma** is the process by which followers convert personal charismatic rule into a lasting organization.

It may involve:

- Selecting a successor
- Writing down teachings
- Creating offices
- Establishing membership rules
- Developing regular sources of funding
- Turning personal commands into procedures

Routinization solves the succession problem but changes the character of the authority. It often transforms charisma into traditional or legal-rational rule.

## Legal-rational authority

> **Legal-rational authority rests on belief in the legality of enacted rules and in the right of officials appointed under those rules to issue commands.**

Subjects obey the office, not the officeholder's family identity or personality. The official's powers come from rules and remain limited by them.

### Core features

- Offices have defined jurisdictions.
- Appointment follows an established procedure.
- Written rules govern decisions.
- Officials require relevant qualifications.
- Hierarchies establish supervision and appeal.
- Records preserve institutional continuity.
- Public and private resources are formally separated.
- A successor receives the office's powers through procedure, not personal inheritance.

### New example: food-safety inspection

A food-safety inspector closes a factory after laboratory tests show contamination above a published statutory limit.

The command is legal-rational because:

1. Legislation creates the inspector's office.
2. Rules define the contamination threshold.
3. The official follows a prescribed testing procedure.
4. The factory can appeal to a designated body.
5. Another qualified inspector in the same office could issue the same order.

The factory obeys the legally defined office, not the inspector as a private person.

### Legal-rational does not automatically mean democratic

Legal-rational authority may exist in democracies and non-democracies. Codified rules, bureaucracy, and valid appointment make authority legal-rational. Democracy additionally requires political equality, participation, accountability, contestation, and protected rights.

Likewise, a rule can be procedurally valid yet substantively discriminatory. Legality identifies a source and procedure. It does not settle legitimacy or justice.

## Mixed authority

Real institutions often combine Weber's types.

### New example: hereditary environmental trust

A coastal trust is chaired by a hereditary custodian, attracts donations through the custodian's personal reputation, and employs scientists under formal service rules.

- Hereditary chairmanship is traditional.
- Personal devotion to the custodian is charismatic.
- Staff appointments and permit procedures are legal-rational.
- Scientific recommendations carry epistemic authority.

The ideal types let us identify each source without forcing the whole institution into one category.

## Scope, limits, and accountability

Authority always has a domain. Legal-rational authority makes that domain especially visible through jurisdiction.

To identify scope, ask:

- Who created the office?
- Which persons and territory fall under it?
- What decisions may it make?
- Which procedure must it follow?
- How long does the authority last?
- Who reviews or reverses its decisions?

### New example: library director

A library director may set opening hours under university regulations. The same office does not authorize the director to inspect students' private messages. The officeholder's general importance cannot enlarge a specific legal jurisdiction.

Traditional and charismatic authority often have less precise boundaries. This makes accountability more difficult because no clear text identifies where the claim ends.

## De jure and de facto authority

### De jure authority

> **De jure authority is authority possessed by law or under a formally valid rule.**

An official may hold de jure authority even when their commands are ignored.

### De facto authority

> **De facto authority is effective command or generally accepted rule exercised in practice, whether or not law grants it.**

A de facto authority may lack a lawful title. Conversely, a de jure officeholder may have little control on the ground.

| Situation | De jure | De facto |
|---|---:|---:|
| Lawful officeholder who effectively governs | Yes | Yes |
| Lawful officeholder whose administration has collapsed | Yes | No or weak |
| Unrecognized actor whose directions are regularly followed | No | Yes |
| Private individual with neither title nor effective command | No | No |

### New example: remote settlement

Law assigns emergency coordination to an elected county officer. A landslide cuts the settlement off, and residents instead follow a volunteer mechanic who controls the only working radio and organizes supplies.

The county officer retains de jure authority. The mechanic acquires temporary de facto authority. Whether the mechanic's directions are normatively legitimate depends on necessity, competence, fairness, and accountability.

> [!note]
> De jure does not always equal legal-rational in every analytical setting, and de facto does not automatically mean charismatic or traditional. One pair identifies the legal status of rule. Weber's types identify the belief that legitimizes it.

## Epistemic and practical authority

The lecture briefly recognizes authority based on knowledge.

- **Epistemic authority** gives others a reason to believe a claim because the speaker has relevant expertise.
- **Practical authority** gives others a reason or duty to act because the authority issues a directive.

### New example: volcanic eruption

A volcanologist has epistemic authority concerning eruption risk. An emergency commissioner may have practical authority to order evacuation. Good administration connects them, but their roles remain different. Scientific expertise alone does not create a legal power to requisition buses, while legal office alone does not make an official's geological prediction reliable.

## Legitimacy

> **Legitimacy is the accepted or justified rightfulness of a ruler, institution, decision, or exercise of power.**

Legitimacy performs two analytical tasks:

1. It explains why people accept rule.
2. It evaluates whether coercive rule is justified.

These correspond to descriptive and normative legitimacy.

## Descriptive legitimacy

> **Descriptive legitimacy exists when people actually believe that an authority or exercise of power is rightful.**

This is an empirical question. Evidence may include willing compliance, public statements, institutional trust, accepted succession, or routine cooperation.

Weber mainly uses legitimacy in this sociological sense. Traditional, charismatic, and legal-rational authority name different beliefs that sustain obedience.

Descriptive legitimacy can vary:

- Across social groups
- Across regions
- Between domestic and international audiences
- Between an institution and one of its decisions
- Over time

Acceptance is not a simple majority count. A bare poll cannot show whether opinions are informed, stable, free, or formed through public reasoning.

## Normative legitimacy

> **Normative legitimacy exists when political power meets an appropriate standard of justification.**

Possible standards include:

- Valid consent
- Protection of basic rights
- Fair procedures
- Political equality
- Public justification
- Effective provision of essential public goods
- Opportunity to contest decisions

Normative legitimacy asks whether rule **should** be accepted, not merely whether it is accepted.

### Legitimacy and justice

Legitimacy is often a lower threshold than complete justice.

- **Legitimacy** asks whether an institution may permissibly rule and enforce decisions.
- **Justice** asks whether its overall arrangement of rights, opportunities, benefits, and burdens is fair.

A state may be legitimate enough to issue binding rules while remaining seriously unjust in some policies. This distinction prevents an all-or-nothing analysis.

## Legality, legitimacy, and justice

| Concept | Main test |
|---|---|
| **Legality** | Was the rule made and applied according to valid law? |
| **Descriptive legitimacy** | Do relevant people accept it as rightful? |
| **Normative legitimacy** | Can its use of political power be justified? |
| **Justice** | Does it treat persons and distribute burdens fairly? |

### New example: predictive school admissions

A public university lawfully adopts an admissions algorithm. Many applicants trust it because it appears neutral. An audit later shows that its training data consistently disadvantages rural schools.

- The policy may be legal.
- It may initially possess descriptive legitimacy.
- The biased procedure weakens normative legitimacy.
- Its unequal effects create a question of justice.

One label cannot answer all four questions.

## Legitimation

> **Legitimation is the process through which a claim or exercise of power comes to be accepted or justified as rightful.**

Legitimacy is a status or judgment. Legitimation is a process.

The lecture identifies several routes:

### 1. Consent

People authorize an institution or accept its rule. Consent is strongest when it is informed, voluntary, specific, and revocable through known procedures.

### 2. Political justification

Authorities provide public reasons for a policy. A mere slogan is not enough. The reasons must address the actual purpose, evidence, costs, alternatives, and distribution of burdens.

### 3. Public benefit

A utilitarian or functional argument justifies authority by the security, coordination, or welfare it produces. Public benefit cannot automatically excuse rights violations. Who receives the benefit and who bears the cost still matter.

### 4. Deliberation

Affected people exchange reasons, question evidence, propose alternatives, and revise positions. Deliberation differs from consultation that merely collects opinions after a decision is complete.

### New example: urban reservoir restrictions

A city faces a severe water shortage.

- A unilateral restriction announced without data offers only a top-down justification.
- Publishing reservoir levels and distribution forecasts makes the reasons testable.
- Meetings with households, hospitals, farmers, and small businesses introduce affected knowledge.
- Revising exemptions after criticism demonstrates deliberation.
- Equal minimum access and an appeal procedure improve normative legitimacy.

The policy's popularity remains an empirical matter. The quality of justification is a normative matter.

## Who recognizes legitimacy?

Legitimacy operates at several scales:

- The persons directly subject to a command
- A professional or religious community
- Citizens of a state
- Constitutional courts and oversight bodies
- Other states and international institutions

An action may be accepted at one scale and rejected at another. Analysis must specify the relevant audience rather than claiming vaguely that "society" approves.

## Can an authority act illegitimately?

Yes. A valid office can misuse its power.

It is clearer to say:

> **An authority exercised power illegitimately.**

rather than:

> **The actor had illegitimate authority.**

The first formulation preserves the distinction between the office's source and the quality of a particular action.

Political philosophy also uses the expression **de facto authority without legitimate authority** for an actor whose commands are effective but whose right to rule fails a normative test. The vocabulary differs by theoretical context, so an answer should define its usage.

### New example: disease-control database

A health ministry has authority to track a contagious disease. It secretly retains location records for unrelated political profiling after the emergency ends. The ministry remains a lawful authority, but the new use exceeds the public purpose that justified collection. The act may be illegal, illegitimate, or both.

## Majoritarianism is not democracy

Majority support can contribute to descriptive legitimacy, but it does not make every policy democratic.

A democratic legitimacy test also asks:

- Were minorities free to speak and organize?
- Were votes and procedures fair?
- Were basic rights protected?
- Could evidence and reasons be challenged?
- Did officials remain within their jurisdiction?
- Could the decision be reviewed?

A majority cannot create unlimited authority merely through approval.

## Political obligation

> **Political obligation is the legal or moral duty of a person to obey the state or another political authority.**

The central problem is not whether disobedience will be punished. It is whether the authority's directive gives the subject a duty or special reason to comply.

### Compliance, obedience, and obligation

- **Compliance:** doing what a directive requires.
- **Obedience:** complying because the recognized authority issued it.
- **Obligation:** possessing a duty to comply.

A person may comply only to avoid a fine. Another may follow the same rule for an independent moral reason. Their behaviour matches, but the reasons differ.

## Legal and moral obligation

### Legal obligation

A legal obligation arises from a valid rule within a legal system. Courts or administrators may enforce it.

### Moral obligation

A moral obligation arises from reasons about right and wrong that do not depend solely on enactment.

| Relationship | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Legal and moral duties agree | Law requires conduct that independent moral reasons also support |
| Legal duty without moral support | Law requires conduct a person regards as morally wrong |
| Moral duty without legal requirement | Morality supports conduct that law does not demand |
| Conflict between duties | Following law would violate a weightier moral duty |

### New example: accessible emergency shelter

Building rules require a manager to keep an emergency exit unlocked. That is a legal obligation. During an evacuation, the manager also has a moral duty to assist a wheelchair user even if no detailed rule assigns that exact task. If an official orders the manager to lock the exit to keep reporters outside, legal and moral reasons support refusal.

## Grounds of political obligation

### Consent theory

Consent theories hold that people become subject to authority by authorizing it.

- **Express consent** is directly stated.
- **Tacit consent** is inferred from conduct.

Tacit consent is weak when refusal is unclear, dangerous, or prohibitively costly. Remaining in one's country cannot automatically count as free consent when leaving would mean abandoning family, language, work, and property.

### Social contract

The social contract explains political authority through an agreement to leave an insecure condition and establish common rules.

- For **Hobbes**, a powerful sovereign secures peace and protection.
- For **Locke**, authority remains limited by natural rights and the purposes for which people transfer power.

The contract is a model of justification, not usually a claim that every citizen signed a historical document.

### Fair play

People who accept benefits from a cooperative scheme may owe their fair share of its burdens. The argument is strongest when the benefits are genuinely necessary, the scheme is fair, and burdens are proportionate.

### Public benefit and coordination

Authority may be justified because some urgent goods require coordinated action. Disease control, flood management, and common infrastructure fail if everyone follows a private rule.

The argument has limits. Producing benefits does not permit an authority to impose any burden by any means.

### Natural duty of justice

People may have a moral duty to support reasonably just institutions, even without personal consent. The duty depends on the institution being sufficiently just and effective.

### Associative obligation

Membership in a political community may create duties resembling responsibilities within other valuable relationships. Critics ask whether political membership is sufficiently voluntary and equal to ground such duties.

## Authority does not create a blanket duty

Recognizing a government as legitimate does not settle every command.

A citizen may:

- Accept the constitutional order
- Recognize an office's authority
- Regard most laws as binding
- Still reject one directive as beyond jurisdiction or gravely unjust

Legitimacy of the state, legitimacy of a law, and obligation in a particular case must be assessed separately.

## A complete worked application

### New case: mandatory evacuation from a volcanic island

Scientists predict an eruption. An elected island council orders evacuation, gives public reasons, provides ferries, and creates a review process for residents who operate essential services.

**Power**

The council controls ferries, police, alerts, and shelters. These resources give it capacity to influence conduct.

**Authority**

Emergency legislation authorizes the council to order evacuation. Volcanologists possess epistemic authority over risk; the council possesses practical authority over movement and public resources.

**Weberian type**

The council's authority is mainly legal-rational because enacted rules define its office, jurisdiction, procedure, and review.

**De jure and de facto**

It has de jure authority under emergency law and de facto authority if residents and agencies follow its directions.

**Descriptive legitimacy**

Trust, voluntary cooperation, and acceptance across settlements indicate actual belief in the order's rightfulness.

**Normative legitimacy**

Evidence, proportionality, equal transport, time limits, and appeal help justify coercion.

**Obligation**

Residents have a legal duty under emergency law. They also have moral reasons not to endanger rescue workers or consume scarce rescue capacity.

**Limit**

If officials reserve ferries for political supporters, the discriminatory implementation weakens legitimacy even if the evacuation order itself remains justified.

## How to analyze an exam problem

1. Define power and identify the practical resources involved.
2. Identify the claimed authority and its jurisdiction.
3. Classify the source using Weber's ideal types.
4. Ask whether authority is de jure, de facto, or both.
5. Separate epistemic expertise from practical authority.
6. Test descriptive legitimacy through actual acceptance.
7. State the normative standard used to judge legitimacy.
8. Separate legality from legitimacy and justice.
9. Identify the proposed process of legitimation.
10. Distinguish legal from moral obligation.
11. Explain whether the directive creates obedience, mere compliance, or justified refusal.

## Key terms

- **Authority:** Recognized or justified right to issue and enforce binding directives.
- **Domination:** Probability that a command will receive obedience.
- **Legitimate domination:** Weber's term for domination accepted as valid by followers.
- **Ideal type:** Analytical model that isolates defining features; it is not a perfect description or moral ideal.
- **Traditional authority:** Authority grounded in the sanctity of inherited custom.
- **Gerontocracy:** Traditional rule by elders.
- **Patriarchalism:** Traditional authority of a household or lineage head.
- **Patrimonialism:** Administration organized as an extension of the ruler's household.
- **Charismatic authority:** Authority grounded in devotion to an individual's exceptional qualities or mission.
- **Routinization of charisma:** Conversion of personal charismatic rule into stable offices, traditions, and procedures.
- **Legal-rational authority:** Authority grounded in enacted rules and valid appointment to an impersonal office.
- **Jurisdiction:** Defined area, persons, and subject matter over which an authority may act.
- **De jure authority:** Authority possessed under law.
- **De facto authority:** Effective or generally obeyed authority in practice.
- **Epistemic authority:** Credibility based on relevant knowledge or expertise.
- **Practical authority:** Capacity to issue directives that claim to give reasons or duties for action.
- **Legitimacy:** Accepted or justified rightfulness of authority or coercive power.
- **Descriptive legitimacy:** Actual belief that rule is rightful.
- **Normative legitimacy:** Rightfulness according to an appropriate standard of justification.
- **Legitimation:** Process through which power becomes accepted or justified as rightful.
- **Legality:** Conformity with valid law.
- **Justice:** Fair arrangement of rights, opportunities, benefits, and burdens.
- **Consent:** Voluntary authorization of another's rule.
- **Tacit consent:** Consent inferred from conduct under conditions permitting meaningful refusal.
- **Deliberation:** Reasoned exchange in which participants can question, propose, and revise.
- **Political obligation:** Legal or moral duty to obey political authority.
- **Compliance:** Acting as directed, whatever the reason.
- **Obedience:** Acting because an authority issued the directive.
- **Fair play:** Duty to bear a fair share of a cooperative scheme's burdens.
- **Natural duty of justice:** Duty to support reasonably just institutions.
