# State and Government: Features and Functions

> [!summary]
> A modern state is generally identified by its **population, territory, sovereignty, government, public institutions, and international recognition**. Government is only one component of the state: it is the temporary, formal, and publicly accountable machinery that makes and implements collective decisions. Michael Mann complements this institutional definition by examining the ideological, economic, military, and political functions states perform.

## Normative and Empirical Analysis

The distinction introduced in [[polsc1|Introduction to Political Science]] remains central to studying the state and government.

| Approach | Main question | Character |
|---|---|---|
| **Normative** | What *should* political life be like? | Prescribes values, ideals, or future arrangements |
| **Empirical** | What exists, and how does it work in practice? | Observes, measures, compares, and evaluates evidence |

A claim is normative whenever it assigns value or prescribes what ought to happen. Normative claims can be grounded in evidence, but evidence alone does not settle which goals are desirable.

### How the Two Approaches Interact

Marx illustrates their relationship:

1. He empirically examined the historical concentration of wealth among a minority.
2. He used this analysis to support the normative goal of a more equal society.

The social-contract theories in [[polsc2|The State: Theories and Critiques]] are also normative:

- **Hobbes:** the state should be a powerful Leviathan that secures order.
- **Locke:** the state should remain limited and protect life, liberty, and property.
- **Rousseau:** the state should represent the general will and may be replaced when it ceases to do so.

Locke and Hobbes demonstrate that normative theories can recommend opposite arrangements. Empirical inquiry can then compare their consequences, but it must still ask which outcome is being valued. Evidence that totalitarian governments are stable, for example, does not establish that totalitarianism is good or that stability is government's only purpose.

> [!important] Operationalization
> Empirical research must translate abstract concepts into observable indicators. Before claiming to measure democracy, participation, inequality, or the general will, a researcher must explain what counts as evidence of that concept.

An app's designers might treat upvotes and downvotes as evidence of democratic participation. High usage would empirically demonstrate interaction with those buttons, but it would not by itself prove that users are democratically empowered. Democracy also involves representation, deliberation, rights, accountability, and other forms of participation.

The same problem applies to computational social science: selecting a metric is never conceptually neutral. Researchers must justify why their chosen indicators adequately represent the political concept under study.

## Scope of the State Model

The characteristics below are a general empirical framework used in political science to identify **modern states**, especially modern liberal-democratic states. They are not universal descriptions of every historical kingdom, empire, colony, authoritarian regime, or disputed territory.

Border disputes, occupations, civil wars, colonies, and partially recognized territories reveal that these features are often matters of degree or contestation. The framework is therefore a baseline for analysis, not a claim that every case fits neatly.

## Defining Features of a State

### 1. Population

A state requires a population: a legally and politically recognized body of members over whom it exercises authority.

Population involves more than the presence of people. It raises questions of:

- **Citizenship:** who is legally recognized as a member?
- **Membership:** which rights and duties follow from belonging?
- **Size:** is there an ideal or sustainable population?
- **Entry and exit:** how can citizenship be acquired, transferred, or lost?
- **Population policy:** does the state encourage or discourage population growth?

Visitors may temporarily fall under a state's jurisdiction without becoming members of it. Conversely, citizens may remain members while living abroad.

The citizenship-by-investment programme of **Saint Kitts and Nevis** illustrates that legal membership can sometimes be acquired through financial contribution. This prompts a broader question: does possession of a passport alone amount to substantive political membership?

### 2. Boundary and Territory

A state claims authority over a geographically identifiable territory. Boundaries determine:

- Where the state's laws apply
- Which people and resources fall under its jurisdiction
- Where another state's authority begins
- What territory the state is expected to administer and protect

Boundaries may result from geography, colonization, war, treaties, or administrative decisions. Straight borders in parts of Africa and the Radcliffe Line in South Asia show how mapmaking and colonial rule shaped modern territories.

#### Enclaves and Everyday Borders

The former India-Bangladesh enclaves showed how complicated territorial membership can become. Residents of one country's enclave inside another country could need to cross an international border to access their own state's public services, sometimes requiring documents that were themselves difficult to obtain. The 2015 Land Boundary Agreement exchanged most of these enclaves and simplified the border.

Formal boundaries also cut through everyday life. People may live on one side while farming, trading, or maintaining family relationships on the other. Territory is therefore both a legal claim and a lived political arrangement.

Disputed cases such as Israel-Palestine and Kashmir demonstrate that territorial boundaries can remain politically and internationally contested.

### 3. Sovereignty

**Sovereignty** means that the state possesses the highest public authority within its jurisdiction and is not legally subordinate to another state.

It has two dimensions:

- **Internal sovereignty:** supreme authority over people and institutions within the territory
- **External sovereignty:** political independence from outside rule

In constitutional democracies, public authority is organized through a supreme constitutional order. In India, the Constitution begins by locating constituent authority in **"We, the People,"** while the Constitution functions as the supreme legal framework.

Sovereignty distinguishes states from dependent territories and administrative regions:

- **Réunion** and **Martinique** are overseas departments and regions of France, not sovereign states.
- **Hong Kong** and **Macau** are special administrative regions under Chinese sovereignty.
- **Western Sahara** has contested sovereignty and territorial control.
- **Taiwan** operates with many practical attributes of a sovereign state but has contested international status and limited formal recognition.

> [!note]
> Autonomy is not the same as sovereignty. A territory may administer many of its own affairs while remaining legally subordinate to a higher authority.

Claims by self-declared "sovereign citizens" or micronations do not create sovereignty when an existing state retains legal authority over the people and territory involved.

### 4. Government

A state requires a government capable of administering its population and territory. Government gives organized and continuing effect to the state's authority.

When governmental institutions cannot effectively administer territory, maintain order, or deliver basic functions, the state may be described as **fragile** or, more controversially, a **failed state**. These labels must be used carefully because authority may be uneven rather than entirely absent.

> [!important]
> The government is a component of the state, not a synonym for it.

### 5. Public Institutions

Government depends on a larger network of institutions rather than only a leader, cabinet, or military. These include:

- Legislature
- Executive
- Judiciary
- Bureaucracy
- Ministries and regulatory bodies
- Taxation and financial institutions
- Police, military, and other security bodies
- Agencies responsible for infrastructure and public services

They are **public** because they are constituted through public authority, supported by public resources, and intended to perform collective functions. A service does not cease to have a public character merely because a private contractor delivers it.

The lecture's hypothetical corporate state exposes this distinction. If an organization taxes a population, builds collective infrastructure, regulates membership, and governs a territory, relabelling citizens as customers and taxes as revenue does not remove the public nature of its functions.

#### New Public Management

**New Public Management (NPM)** became influential in the 1980s and 1990s. It proposed that government should adopt private-sector methods by emphasizing:

- Efficiency and measurable performance
- Competition and contracting out
- Privatized service delivery
- Citizens understood partly as consumers
- Managerial rather than ideological decision-making

NPM does not eliminate government; it changes how public functions are organized and evaluated. Its limits become visible when profitability or consumer choice conflicts with equality, universal access, or democratic accountability.

### 6. International Recognition

Modern statehood also depends partly on recognition by other states and participation in the international system. This feature became especially significant after the Second World War and the creation of the **United Nations**.

Recognition indicates that other states accept an entity as an independent member of international society. It affects:

- Diplomatic relations
- Treaty-making
- International organization membership
- Passports and citizenship
- Trade and legal interaction

Recognition is not always uniform. Israel and Palestine are recognized by different combinations of states, while Taiwan has extensive practical international relations despite limited diplomatic recognition.

A state may also recognize another state while rejecting or condemning its current government. Recognition does not imply friendship, approval, alliance, or protection from attack.

## Applying the Criteria

| Feature | Guiding question |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Is there an identifiable political membership? |
| **Territory** | Is there a territory over which authority is claimed? |
| **Sovereignty** | Is the entity the highest independent authority within that territory? |
| **Government** | Is there an organized body capable of governing? |
| **Public institutions** | Are there durable institutions that exercise and administer public authority? |
| **International recognition** | Do other states accept it as a member of the international system? |

No single difficult case can be resolved mechanically. A disputed entity may satisfy some criteria strongly and others only partially.

### Are a Military and Economy Separate Criteria?

A military and an economy are important to most states, but the lecture does not treat them as separate defining features.

- Military capacity falls under sovereignty, government, and public institutions.
- Economic management falls under government and public institutions.
- States differ widely in military organization and economic capacity without necessarily losing statehood.

Military institutions typically protect territory and population from external threats, though states have also deployed them internally. International recognition does not make military conflict impossible, nor does disagreement eliminate recognition.

## Michael Mann: Four Sources of State Power

Political sociologist **Michael Mann** shifts attention from what features identify a state to what forms of power states organize. His **IEMP model** distinguishes four interacting sources of social power.

| Source | State function | Core concern |
|---|---|---|
| **Ideological** | Promotes and reproduces an official way of understanding the political community | Identity, values, legitimacy, and shared meaning |
| **Economic** | Organizes, regulates, and influences production, markets, taxation, and distribution | Resources and material organization |
| **Military** | Protects territory and concentrates organized coercive force | Defence, security, and physical force |
| **Political** | Centralizes and exercises authoritative rule over a territory | Governance, administration, and political power |

Ideology here does not refer only to named doctrines such as capitalism, socialism, or Marxism. Every state advances some account of its identity, values, institutions, and legitimate social order.

> [!tip] Features versus functions
> The six criteria ask **whether an entity can be identified as a state**. Mann's model asks **how states organize and exercise power**. The frameworks complement rather than replace each other.

## State and Government Are Not Synonyms

The terms must remain analytically separate:

| State | Government |
|---|---|
| Broader and relatively permanent political order | Temporary body and machinery that governs |
| Includes population, territory, sovereignty, and institutions | One institutional component of the state |
| Continues across elections and changes of leadership | Changes with elections, appointments, coups, or other transitions |
| Not identical to a country, nation, society, or economy | Not identical to the party currently in office |

The popular or journalistic use of *government* often means the current ruling party or administration, such as the "Modi government." Political science also uses a broader meaning: the enduring collection of institutions and processes through which public decisions are made and carried out.

> [!important] Political consequence
> Criticizing or opposing a government is not necessarily criticism or rejection of the state. A temporary administration and the continuing political order are distinct objects.

## Defining Features of Government

### 1. Public and Formal Institutions and Processes

Government is not one person or organization. It is a set of **public, formal institutions and processes**.

- **Public** means established through public authority and directed toward collective affairs.
- **Formal** means constituted and constrained by codified rules, laws, procedures, or constitutional provisions.

Rules allocate responsibilities among institutions. For example, economic governance is divided among the Ministry of Finance, the Reserve Bank of India, and other bodies, with their relationships and powers defined by law.

#### Formality, Constitutionalism, and Accountability

Formality makes official action traceable. An institution should be able to identify:

- The legal source of its authority
- The procedure it followed
- The official responsible for the decision
- The mechanism through which the action can be reviewed or challenged

This connects formality to **constitutionalism** and **accountability**. An official action taken outside authorized procedure is not legitimized merely because a government employee performed it.

### 2. Making Laws and Decisions

Government makes collectively binding laws and policy decisions. It can also reorganize its own institutions, but must do so through the procedures authorized by the constitutional and legal order.

### 3. Implementing Laws and Decisions

Government must translate decisions into practice. Administration includes:

- Creating rules and programmes
- Allocating budgets and personnel
- Delivering services
- Enforcing requirements
- Monitoring whether policy goals are met

Law-making without implementation does not achieve the law's intended public purpose.

### 4. Regulating Public and Private Institutions

Government regulates its own agencies as well as private bodies operating within the state. A business, corporation, university, or residential association may create internal rules, but those rules remain subordinate to applicable public law.

### 5. Exercising Power and Authority

Government possesses both **power** and **authority**. Officials can make decisions, issue orders, enforce laws, and determine legal eligibility because formal rules assign them these capacities.

Authority is not supposed to be personal or arbitrary. Its legitimate scope comes from the office, law, and procedure rather than merely from the individual holding power. Power and authority are treated as separate concepts later in the course.

### 6. Representing the People's Interests

Normatively, government should act for the population's benefit and represent its interests or general will. This differs from the preceding empirical characteristics because governments do not always represent their populations equally or democratically.

The claim remains important as a standard by which government can be evaluated:

- Whose interests shape decisions?
- Who participates and who is excluded?
- Are institutions responsive and accountable?
- Do public benefits reach the population?

## Normative Models of Government

Different theories prescribe different scopes and purposes for government.

| Model | Normative position |
|---|---|
| **Minimal or laissez-faire state** | Government should protect basic rights and order while interfering as little as possible in social and economic life. |
| **Anarchism** | Society does not require a state or ruler; this does not necessarily mean the absence of rules or social organization. |
| **Totalitarian state** | State power should extend across political, economic, and social life, seeking comprehensive control. |
| **New Public Management** | Government should be run using corporate and market-oriented methods, with greater privatization and performance measurement. |

The models differ over whether government should be limited, abolished, made comprehensive, or managed like a corporation. They are normative because each proposes how governing institutions ought to be organized.

## Exam-Ready Synthesis

1. Normative analysis asks what the state or government ought to be; empirical analysis studies and measures how political arrangements operate.
2. A modern state is generally identified through population, territory, sovereignty, government, public institutions, and international recognition.
3. These criteria are analytical baselines, and disputed or dependent territories may satisfy them only partially.
4. Military and economic capacities matter but are contained within broader features rather than treated as independent requirements.
5. Michael Mann's IEMP model describes the ideological, economic, military, and political sources of state power.
6. Government is one component of the state: governments are temporary, while the state is comparatively permanent.
7. Government consists of public and formal institutions that make decisions, implement laws, regulate institutions, and exercise legally assigned power and authority.
8. Representing the people's interests is a normative expectation, not a feature realized equally by all governments.
9. Minimalism, anarchism, totalitarianism, and New Public Management prescribe sharply different roles for government.

## Key Terms

- **State:** A sovereign political order governing a population within a territory through public institutions and recognized within the international system.
- **Population:** The body of people subject to the state, including its legally recognized members or citizens.
- **Territory:** The geographical area over which a state claims jurisdiction.
- **Sovereignty:** Supreme internal public authority combined with external political independence.
- **Government:** The formal institutions and processes through which a state is governed.
- **Public institution:** An institution constituted through public authority to perform collective functions.
- **International recognition:** Acceptance of an entity's statehood by other states.
- **Failed state:** A contested label for a state whose institutions cannot effectively perform core governing functions.
- **Operationalization:** Converting an abstract concept into observable and measurable indicators.
- **IEMP model:** Mann's framework of ideological, economic, military, and political sources of social power.
- **Constitutionalism:** Government according to a higher framework of rules that organizes and limits public power.
- **Accountability:** The obligation to explain and justify official conduct and remain subject to review or sanction.
- **Minimal state:** A state restricted mainly to protecting rights, property, and order.
- **Anarchism:** A family of theories rejecting rulers or the state while permitting non-state forms of social organization.
- **Totalitarianism:** A model in which the state seeks comprehensive control over public and private life.
- **New Public Management:** Application of corporate, market, and performance-oriented methods to public administration.
